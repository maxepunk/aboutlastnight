The Room Convicts the Reporter

Offered the same money as everyone, Ashe Motoko maintained her journalistic integrity, even exposing a memory that could be construed as motive. Now she's accused of murder. Who stands to benefit from this turn of events?

Cassandra Nova | NovaNews | Senior Investigative Correspondent || Ashe Motoko, Taylor Chase | Guest Reporter, Contributing Reporter · February 22, 2027 · Article NNA-101126-26

Ashe Motoko had already filed seven memories to me from inside the warehouse when Blake gathered everyone to take the room’s temperature, and she filed two more. Someone told her, “You should probably bury those, Ashe. I don't think those need to see the light of day.” Ashe: “All I want is the truth.” Blake told the room he had made her the same offer to bury that others had received: “I know writing puzzles for a daily doesn't really make a lot of money...” She kept reporting. At 8:39 AM, this reached me:

Ashe Confronts Taylor
You uncovered a lot of secrets in your research on BizAI, but thanks to the NDA your lawyer made you sign, they’ve stayed secrets. If your expose had been published, and the truth had come out about MARCUS, it would have been worth it to lose your job at the SVBJ. But somebody tipped MARCUS off before your article went to print, and he was able to pull some strings and bury it. You just spotted that someone sitting across the room. TAYLOR ruined your career, and you’re not going to stay silent about how you feel.
Ashe Motoko

“Wait, that incriminates you,” someone said. Blake agreed: “It kinda does. It kinda gives you some motive about why you might hate Marcus enough to kill him, but all right.” Ashe said she had nothing to hide, and the memory went into the record with the rest of her reporting.

My questions start with that card. Before the police arrived, the room named Ashe as Marcus Blackwood’s killer: five votes to Jess Kane’s three, with two for Sarah Blackwood. Blake: “Ashe, I'm really sorry.”

Blake had opened the deliberation by crediting “Ashe Motoko, intrepid journalist,” who “has done quite a bit of investigation and put up quite a bit of evidence.” The twelve memories she filed from the room are her reporting for this article, and the reason she shares its byline. She was chasing Marcus long before the party. Writing to Nat Francisco this month about an exposé on him that an NDA had buried, she put it simply: “Someone needs to get the truth out.”

In a room where millions bought silence, the reporter who wouldn’t stop reporting is the one handed to the police. So here is the question I am chasing: who gains when the journalist whose work could bring NeurAI down is tied up in a murder case?

What the Reporter Exposed

Ashe’s memory says somebody tipped Marcus off. On Jan 31, 2027, Taylor Chase wrote to him: “Done. Ashe's expose on your previous company won't ever see daylight.” And: “They'll be reassigned to lifestyle by Monday.” Taylor reported from the room this morning too, filing eight memories, and shares this byline as a contributing reporter. Her first reached me at 8:50 AM. Ashe’s memory of confronting her had been on the board since 8:39.

Ashe’s reporting sketches what NeurAI was. At 8:23 AM she filed Nat’s memory:

Marcus Lied About NeurAI’s AI
You, NAT and SAM gather together to talk about what to do with MARCUS. You’ve tried to convince those two to stop enabling him for years. But at this point, he might be too far gone. Now you’re learning that he’s lied about the AI that underpins his company. It doesn’t work. But instead of fixing it, he’s taking drugs and inventing techniques to steal people’s memories instead? Maybe his high pressure deal with CyberLife pushed him over the edge and into this weird, out-of-the-box thinking….
Nat Francisco

At 8:46 she filed her own memory of Zia Bishara handing her a scoop: Marcus had asked to install Zia’s algorithm “into NeurAI’s code base ‘temporarily’ so he wouldn’t bomb a demo with CyberLife.” Zia’s own memory, which Taylor filed at 9:13, complicates the word stolen: “And you relented, because MARCUS has helped you out of a jam before, and EZRA taught you to collaborate.” Lent, then, for one demo. The system it surfaced in was later called ‘proprietary.’

The first memory anyone turned in, anonymously at 7:42 AM, was Marcus’s own, and it begins: “The extractions worked.” Remi Whitman spent the morning unlocking memories and was crucial to cracking the hidden UV symbols on the documents. Her own memory went up anonymously at 9:11:

The Data Is Identical
You turn SAM’s laptop around so that VIC and ALEX can both see the code comparison. On the left: ZIA’s Oracle Ledger commit from 2023. On the right: NeurAI's 'proprietary' system from 2027. the data is identical. MARCUS didn't even bother changing the notes. He’s not just a thief, he’s a sloppy thief. Next you roll the demo. That’s even sloppier. Maybe now VIC will stop prioritizing NeurAI and give you the time of day.
Remi Whitman

Kai Andersen’s memory, anonymous at 9:08, finds Cass Zhang’s code in the installation Kai built for Marcus’s party, and asks: “What makes this program worth stealing and executing on unsuspecting guests?” The baggies from Marcus’s back room say what went into those guests: “Psychotrophin3B is the formulation that was used to extract party-goers’ memories on Feb 21, 2027, the night of Marcus’ death.” Blake put it to the room plainly: “I think last night might have been a proof of concept of that.” Marcus’s memory admits the mess: “This is chaos.” And: “Drugged people make terrible assistants.” A proof of concept doesn’t need to be tidy. It needs to work, and by Marcus’s own account, it did.

Follow the Money

While Ashe reported, others sold. At 7:50 AM the first memory was erased: $375,000 into an account called Marcus JR, plus a $50,000 bonus a minute later for going first. At 8:23, in the minute Ashe filed Nat’s memory, $225,000 went into San Francisco. Between the memories she filed at 8:26 and 8:30, Marcus JR took $225,000, $150,000, $450,000 and $450,000. At 8:42, two minutes after the second memory she filed at Blake’s gathering, $450,000 more went into Marcus JR and $225,000 into Party. At 8:57, in the minute Taylor filed three memories, Wow took $250,000, $75,000 and $150,000.

No account carries Ashe’s name and no sale names its seller, so I can’t prove she sold nothing. I can show twelve memories she reported and Blake’s word that the offer was made. That said, I have no reason to believe that she sold a single memory.

At the end, when someone asked whether people would share their account names, Blake read the totals: 47 at $4,215,000; Marcus JR at $2,775,000 (“Anyone want to take credit? No?”); San Francisco at $900,000; Sam at $855,000; Wow at $700,000; Unlock at $475,000; Party at $285,000. In all, $9,455,000 was paid this morning to make memories disappear. I suspect the buyer was NeurAI and its board, the people with the most to lose if any evidence came out that Marcus was not acting alone last night when he ran the extraction protocol. At the vote, Ashe made what I think is a critical observation:

"I don't think it's just about the money."

Ashe Motoko

The Fixer Points at the Press

The deliberation didn’t start with Ashe. Blake had been hearing about an overdose and a suspicious punch, but no names. The punch came first, and Alex Reeves denied it: “I haven't punched anyone since I was in school. I've only been punched.” The last memory to reach the board, turned in anonymously at 9:14 AM, was his own, and contradicted his denial: “Your fist moves before your brain.” Blake let it go: “a punch is not a murder, of course.” Sarah Blackwood, whose evidence pointed to divorce proceedings, answered flatly: “There was no divorce.”

Then Blake reached Morgan Reed, who had offered earlier, “I mean, Sam did it.” Faced with evidence that he was divesting from Marcus, Morgan said: “That's why I, you know, buried all that evidence trying to keep his legacy safe.” Blake: “So you buried evidence?” Morgan: “I'm a fixer. What can I say?” Ashe had reported one of his memories at 8:31 AM:

Morgan Trades the Money Trail
When RILEY approaches, your mood lifts. They’re always a friendly face in the crowd. Except for this time. They come in hot, brandishing a sheaf of papers documenting all the shell companies you set up for MARCUS. They've done their homework. Your name is all over the paper trail. You've seen what happens when this kind of news gets out. You'll have to figure out some way to get ahead of it. But for now, you're beat. You tell RILEY how to trace the money, in exchange for keeping your name out of their accusations.
Morgan Reed

Another, turned in anonymously at 9:02, hardly shows a man protecting Marcus’s name. Morgan tells him, “Go public about the insider trading and take full responsibility or I give all of this damning evidence to Senator Walsh.” Then: “Instead of answering, he reaches into his bag and hands you a generous bribe.” Flip’s memory, filed by Taylor, catches Marcus with “that DC type”: “MARCUS just casually handed over a fat wad of cash like it’s nothing to him.” Flip names no one. Set the two side by side yourself.

Then the fixer turned the room: “You know who is suspicious, though? We found a whole bunch of stuff here saying that there was a reporter here at the party.” Blake called it “pointing the finger at the one journalist who's been exposing a ton of evidence and the other journalist who has been exposing evidence.” Taylor, who had once written to Morgan that “We both know access = power,” owned the feud: “I think it's well known that Ashe and I don't like each other.” Morgan aimed past her:

"I mean, Marcus ruined Ashe's life. That's a really, really good motive right there."

Morgan Reed

The man who said he buried evidence had named the reporter who put his deal on the board.

Ashe went looking for whoever had put up nothing: “Do we have somebody with no memories exposed at all?” Blake saw nothing from Vic Kingsley or Jess Kane. When the accusation came, Ashe said, “No, I have nothing to hide,” and told the room to be cautious of anyone who had hidden every one of their memories. Sam Thorne made her openness the charge: “She also tried to expose everything... That's very suspicious.” Blake: “Oh, a distraction strategy, you think.” Asked for her story, Jess said only, “I don't know.” Blake offered that Jess “seems to have been really, really traumatized by whatever happened last night, I think. She's still recovering.” Vic said she didn’t really know anything and didn’t really do anything.

The accusation had been Morgan’s, with four minutes left: “I think it was Ashe.” Blake: “Morgan is doubling down on Ashe.” The voting resulted in five hands going up for Ashe and three for Jess. Vic drew no votes, cleared as a well-trusted venture capitalist. With 15 seconds left Sarah’s name went up, and drew two. Blake: “Ashe, I'm really sorry.”

The last memory Ashe filed, at 9:02 AM, is her own, and it is the one I keep rereading. Marcus insults her date, Kai, and she can’t risk being thrown out of the party: “You decide the best course of action for all is to run away and pretend this never happened.” It proves nothing about how Marcus died. To my eye, it isn’t the memory of a woman about to kill him, either.

What the Board Never Showed

Vic Kingsley walked out with no memory on the board and no votes. Her paper trail says more. Kingsley-Fund’s first two seed investments were NeurAI and its rival, Remi’s AI Bio Comp; asked why, Vic said, “I have my reasons.” Remi wrote to her in January, “I know you've promised $20 Million to Marcus at NeurAI”. Four days before the party, Sam Thorne’s diary has Marcus saying, “I'm worried about what Vic knows.” What did NeurAI’s backer know about the code she was funding? The room took her at her word.

In the final minutes, Jess Kane was seen walking away with Blake for a private conversation. In the same stretch the morning’s largest account filled: twenty-one sales into 47 at 9:16 and 9:17 AM, six of them at $450,000, minutes before the 9:20 close. The last exposure, Alex’s punch, went up at 9:14, and Blake called for accusations with four minutes left. By those landmarks, my inference is that 47 filled while the room was naming Ashe. The ledger ties no one to 47, Jess included. I would still like to know what she and Blake discussed.

By the time I sat down to write, Ashe Motoko had turned herself in to the police for questioning. Her comment to NovaNews: “I have nothing to hide.”

Here is my read. Marcus had become a liability, his usefulness spent. Ashe was a thorn in NeurAI’s side: a reporter who lost her job when Marcus buried her exposé, but who was still investigating him. In my opinion, she is the victim of a scheme, by the same people responsible for Marcus’s death, to clear away every threat to the technology tested on last night’s guests. And now, with Marcus gone, and Ashe conveniently tied up with a murder investigation, whomever gains control of this memory extraction technology have cleared a path to a broader rollout of a marketplace of stolen memories.

Who profits most from that market, and who paid $9,455,000 this morning to keep last night quiet, is what I am chasing next. I will keep reporting as it develops.
