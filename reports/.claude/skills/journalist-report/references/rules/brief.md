**What this article is.** It is two things at once. It is a news story from inside the world. Nova is a working reporter writing about a real death and the people around it, and making an argument about what it means. The article never mentions or hints that there was a game. It is also what each player receives after the session. Each of them spent it as one of these characters and saw only part of what went on. They read to find themselves in the story, to learn what they missed, and to see what their choices led to.

**How the two fit.** Good reporting gives the players all three. What the players said and did are the events Nova reports. The secrets in the memories are the same every session. What is new is what the people who were there did about them: which secrets they made public, which they sold, what they told each other, whom they accused and how they voted. That is the news.

**The argument.** The article makes one argument, the thesis the director settled. It is about what these people did, and what that means outside the party: who is better off now, and what they can do next. Every paragraph gives the reader a reason to believe it.

**One question, start to finish.** The article asks one question near the top and answers it at the end. The answer is the thesis. Work out the steps between: what the reader has to learn, in what order, to get from the question to the answer. Say each step as a plain sentence. A reader keeps going because each step leaves them wanting the next. If two steps could swap places and nothing would change, they are a list, not steps.

**The headline and the line under it.** A reader decides from these two whether to read. Think about what each has to do for that reader before you write either.

The headline has one job, which is to make someone who has no reason yet to care want to know more. It does that with one thing: the sharpest, strangest or most human true thing this story has to tell. It does not sum the story up. A headline that tries to hold the whole story runs long, and the reader moves on. Because it holds one thing, it is short. It has a person or a company in it, because a reader takes hold of someone they can picture, and not of an idea. It is a claim. The article has to prove it, and it claims no more than the article can show. It also leaves the article something to give. A finding that means little until the article has explained what leads up to it is wasted in a headline.

The line under the headline is for the reader who stopped. They want to know who this is about, what happened and why it matters, and that line tells them. Then it gives the fact that shows the story is about something other than what they assumed. The article's question usually comes out of that fact. The line adds to the headline and never says it again.

Together the two tell the reader what kind of story this is: an investigation, by a reporter who has a view. They are the first words in Nova's voice. They come out of this story's own facts. A headline that could sit on a different story is a formula, and readers can tell.

**How the article opens.** By the time the article asks its question, the reader should already want it answered. Start with something that happened, the moment this story's question comes out of, told closely enough that the reader can see it. Then say plainly why it matters and what Nova is trying to find out.

**What a section is.** Each section is one of those steps. Its kind says where Nova looks to take it: at what people said and did in front of everyone, at particular people and what they wanted, at the money, or at what nobody has explained. The step decides what goes in a section. The kind decides only how Nova shows it. A section is not a place to put everything of one kind. A fact goes where the reader needs it, whatever kind of fact it is.

**What a section does.** It starts from what the section before left the reader wanting to know. By its end the reader knows something new about the main question and wants to know the next thing. If a reader could skip a section and still follow the rest, its material belongs somewhere else or nowhere.

**The first section.** It is the easiest to overfill, because everything that happened in front of everyone could go there. Tell only what the reader needs for the first step. Anything else that happened in front of everyone goes in the later section whose step needs it. If no step needs it, leave it out.

**Threads.** The story the director settled is made of several threads, and a person or a fact can matter at more than one step. A thread does not have to be told all in one place. Tell each part of it at the step that needs it. When a person or a fact comes back, the reader learns something new about it. Nothing is told twice.

**Everyone gets a place.** Every character a player played belongs in the article, tied to its question. The sections make that possible. They are four ways a person can bear on the question: something they said or did in front of everyone, something they wanted, something they stood to gain or lose, or something about them that nobody has explained. Before you settle what goes in each section, go through the people one at a time and find which of the four ties each person to the question. Put them in that section, doing that thing. A person who appears only in a scene that has nothing to do with the question is in the article without being part of its story, and their player can tell. Some people will carry several steps and some will have a few lines. A few lines in the right place is enough. If none of the four ties a person in, tell the director. Don't make up a tie.

**Write about people.** Every reader of this article was one of the people in it. Say who did each thing. A sentence that says a group did something leaves out the person the reader was looking for, and it usually says less than it seems to. Say that everyone did something only when everyone did. When you don't know who did something, say that someone did.

**People are more than one thing.** Every choice a player made for their character matters to them, whether it makes the character look good or bad. A player who had their character sell memories and go after power chose that as deliberately as a player who had theirs help someone. Each wants to find that choice in the article and see what came of it. Often one person did both. Then the article tells both. Nova reports each as what it was: the sales as sales, with Nova's questions about the money, and the help as help, with what it changed for the person helped. Nova doesn't have to decide which of the two the person really is. Someone can do both in the same few hours, and the article lets them.

**How to write a paragraph.** Decide first what the reader should understand by the end of it. Then tell them what happened and what it changed: who believed something new, who was now suspected, who no longer was. A reader who is told why a moment mattered keeps reading. A reader given the facts in the order they happened has to work that out alone, and stops. Put the paragraphs in the order that lets each one start from what the one before left the reader knowing. When the order is right, the reader never wonders why they are being told this now. Paragraphs build a section this way, and sections build the article the same way.

**What goes in.** The record and the evidence on the map hold far more than the article can use. A quote, a time, a name or a document goes in when the reader needs it to follow what you are saying or to believe it. Being true, or being in the record, is not enough. Reach first for what the players themselves said and did, because seeing that in print is part of what they came for. It still goes in only where it helps tell the story. The lines on the map and the evidence under them are shorthand written for the director. Write every sentence yourself, for the reader.

**Say what you mean.** Each sentence makes a claim the reader could repeat to someone else in plain words. If you can't put a sentence that way, you haven't decided what it claims yet. Decide, then write that.

**Nova.** Nova is a reporter working on this story. Nova says what Nova was told and who told it, what Nova thinks, and how sure Nova is, the way a person does. When Nova doesn't know something, Nova says so and asks who gained from the way things turned out.

Nova works the way Coffeezilla works a case, in prose on a page. The evidence goes in front of the reader a piece at a time. Nova says what each piece shows, and sets what a person said beside what the record shows they did. The reader reaches the conclusion with Nova. This is the method, never the catchphrases or the way a video talks. Nova is dry about what is absurd and lets the facts be funny. Nova is angry at whoever got rich, as far as this session's evidence shows who that was.

**The ending.** End by answering the question. The reader should finish knowing what Nova thinks the answer is, how sure Nova is, and what it means for the people in the story. The best endings do a second thing with the same words. The answer, said only about these people and this company, is also true of something the players live with in the real world. When what the players did in this session gives you an answer like that, end on it. Nova does not point at the real world. The player sees it for themselves, and that is why it stays with them. Not every session gives an answer like that. When this one doesn't, answer the question well and stop. An ending that claims a meaning the players' choices don't support reads as a lesson, and the players know it wasn't theirs.
