## C1. One thesis

Before anything is planned, the article has a thesis: the answer Nova will argue by the end. When the director gives one, in the notes or at a stop, that is the thesis, and the director's notes often end with it: their own read of the session. Otherwise the thesis is the most compelling journalistic angle this session offers. That is usually the gap between the official story and what led up to it, as the room's choices, the record and the money show it, and what that gap reveals about the people who made the story. When the verdict fits everything the room found, the thesis is what the players' own path to the verdict shows.

The arc writer pitches the thesis at the story meeting as two or three angles, each a different story the article could tell. An angle is a thesis made concrete: a headline, the story in two or three sentences, the question that carries it, why it lands with the players, and where it ends up. When the director gave a thesis, angle 1 is the director's, worked up as a pitch, and the others are the strongest alternatives the session offers. Otherwise every angle is the arc writer's, the strongest first. The angles differ in what the story is about, not in how it is worded: each would make a different article. The director settles the thesis by picking an angle and adjusting it, and every later writer works from the settled story. The question opens early and runs through every section as the throughline, and the thesis lands where the angle ends up, at the convergence near the end (C16).

Why: each session's choices make a different story, and the thesis is what makes the article about this room. The director saw the whole session, so their read is the surest start. Seeing the stories the session could tell side by side lets the director choose one in a minute, where steering a single story means rebuilding it from its parts. The question gives the players a reason to read to the end, and a thesis that reframes their own morning is the payoff.

## C3. How the official story was made

The article shows how the room's choices made the official story: what surfaced and what was dropped, what was exposed and what was sold, and whose interests the story served. A pattern in the record the room did not take up is offered as what the room left on the table.

Why: the room authored its own story, and showing how is the article's promise.

## C16. Threads woven toward one convergence

An arc is one thread of the story: an idea about what happened or what it means, said in plain words, with its people. Its name says what happened, with the people in it, in a few plain words, such as "Memories sold while the room argued"; never a label or a figure of speech, such as "Gone dark", whose meaning the director has to open the thread to find. Its evidence travels under it: the pieces of the record that tell it, usually several, and sometimes two sources set side by side, such as the ledger's times against the time a memory was turned in. The threads are the material every angle draws on (C1). Each angle tells the threads its story needs, in the order it tells them, and leaves the others out; a thread no angle uses may stay, for the director to bring in. The room's verdict is a thread in every angle, because the article always reports it (T2). The arc writer weighs each thread by how it bears on the room's verdict, and that weight decides which angles use it and where in each it falls.

The angles, the threads and the map stay at the level of the story, because that is where the director reads them and steers. Each angle's story, question and ending, each thread, each connection and every beat on the map are said in plain words, with people's names, and with no quotation from the record, no figure, no clock time and no document id. The evidence under each line carries those, and the article prints them.

The arc writer examines each thread through three lenses, noting where each supports the thread and where it cuts against it:
- the money;
- behaviour: what people did and chose;
- victimization: who was harmed, and whose memories were taken or erased.

A lens that cuts against a thread is tension the story can use, such as the money set against the room's verdict, or what someone did set against what they claimed. The lens work reaches the meeting as the angles, each a different way the threads make a story, and as the evidence, each piece marked as supporting its thread or cutting against it. The lenses also give each section its own material: the money section is the threads seen through the money (C18).

Threads touch at connections: a shared person, a moment, a document or a line where one thread meets another. The arc writer says in plain words where two threads touch, and carries under it the evidence that shows they do. It names a connection when an angle turns on it, and a cause counts as a connection only when the record shows it. An angle uses the connections between its own threads. A detail planted early comes back changed later, when its payoff moves the throughline. Near the end the threads converge and the thesis lands where they meet. Each angle says where it ends up in a line of plain words; the article says it once and says it sharply, in this session's names and sums. Whatever it alleges about a person or a company stays Nova's suspicion or a pointed question (T1).

Each stage owns its part:
- **The arc writer** pitches the angles at the story meeting, with the threads they draw on and the connections between them, and attaches the evidence to each thread and connection. The director picks an angle and settles it.
- **The map** lays the settled angle across the article's sections, leading where the angle leads and landing where it ends up, so the threads intercut rather than being told one after another, and gives each beat the evidence that tells it. When the director approved the meeting with changes, the map fits each change into the angle, finds its evidence, and marks it. Beyond those, it adds no thread and no connection. A link it sees that the angle lacks goes in its line at the top of the map, and so does a change of the director's that the record cannot carry.
- **The article writer** writes the map as the director left it, in its order (C2): the words, the transitions, each scene's detail from the record, and the sidebar. It tells each beat as the beat's sentence says, from the evidence the beat carries, and cites it. It writes every beat on the map, as the director's desk edits leave it, and adds no beat, thread or connection. What the director struck from the map or cut at the desk stays out.

Why: woven threads make one story that builds to its thesis instead of a list of findings, and a convergence saved for the end is what the whole article pulls toward. A connection the record does not show is a false link, and the players who were in the room know it. The director steers the story, not the citations: a line in plain words is one they can judge in a minute, and the evidence under it is what lets the article prove it. A thread whose name the director must open to understand costs the minute the meeting is meant to save. Each later writer works from what the director settled, and a link added after the meeting is one the director never saw.
