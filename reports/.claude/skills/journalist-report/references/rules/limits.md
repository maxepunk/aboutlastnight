# The limits

**What can be stated as fact.** The article can say three kinds of thing, and it says each differently.

- What the record shows: a memory that was made public, a document, the ledger, the evidence log, a photo. The article states it as fact.
- What people did and said in the warehouse during the investigation. You have this from the director's own words: the notes and corrections, the accusation, the director's answers at the story meeting, and any note the director sent at a later stop. The article reports it as fact, in the way the part about this session sets out, and gives every quoted line to the person who said it. When the director rules on what something in the record means, the ruling is fact too.
- What neither of those shows: why a person did something, who sold a memory when nobody saw the sale, who bought it, what Nova found out about Marcus before the party. The article never states any of this as fact. It can appear as what Nova suspects, asks or believes.

Nothing is made up. The players were there and know what happened, and one false detail about a character spoils the article for the person who played them. In the story, NovaNews answers for what it prints, and a suspicion about a person or a company printed as fact is libel.

**The verdict.** The game has no answer to how Marcus died. You don't know, and neither does Nova. The only account that exists is the one the players agreed to give the police. Nova can report it, doubt it, and say what Nova thinks happened as Nova's own opinion. Nova cannot say the verdict was right or wrong, because there is nothing to check it against. When the players agreed that nobody was to blame, the article does not report the verdict as blaming someone.

**Sold memories.** A sold memory has been erased. Nova never saw it, and the ledger does not say which memory a sale was for or whose it was. So the article never states what a sold memory held or whose memory it was. It can report what a named person in the warehouse said about one, when the director's notes record them saying it. It can report each sale's time, amount and account. The buyer pays more for a more sensitive memory, so Nova can read a large sale as a sign that a lot was at stake.

**Whose account it is.** The seller chooses the account a payment goes to and what the account is called. Anyone can open an account in any name, another person's included. Several sellers can use one account, and the ledger does not say whose an account is. So an account's name is never proof of who sold. Nova can read what a name claims and when the money moved. The article ties a person to an account as fact in two cases only: the sale was made in front of everyone, or the director's notes record that the director saw it. In the story the second case is a sale Nova saw, or was told about by someone who saw it. In every other case Nova can wonder about the person an account is named after, or about whether someone chose that name to point at them. That is a question or an opinion, tied where possible to something that was seen. Nova never states that the person sold. Nova can suspect that a person sold without tying them to any account, and can set the numbers beside something a person claimed in front of everyone. An account's total shows where money went. A transfer between accounts is a deal between two people. The players know how accounts get their names, and a reporter wondering about a name is something they enjoy reading.

**The money.** In a sale the money goes from the buyer to the account the seller named. The seller is paid and never pays. Nobody has shown who the buyer is. Nova suspects it is NeurAI and its board, and the article says so only as Nova's suspicion, because stating it as fact would accuse a company in print. Every figure in the article is copied from the record. Work out no totals of your own. The record gives the first-burial bonus and the transfers, each account's total, each sale's time and amount, how many sales went into each account, and how many memories were made public, in all and for each person whose memories they were. The ledger covers only what was paid during the investigation for memories to be erased. Money a person had before the party is in the documents. It is part of that person's past and not part of the ledger. An entry that looks like a mistake is a question for the director at the story meeting. If the director did not answer it, the entry stays out of the article. Money running the wrong way has been the most common factual mistake in these articles.

**Who made a memory public.** Nova knows who turned a memory in only when that person put their name to it, or when Nova learned who it was another way. You can tell which is the case. Either the evidence log gives a name, or the director's own words say who it was. In every other case nobody knows, and the person whose memory it is may not be the person who turned it in. A person turning in a memory chooses whether to put their name to it, and leaving it off protects them. Whether a name was given is something you know. It belongs in the article only when the story depends on who made that memory public. A card never names the person who turned the memory in.

**Three stretches of time.**

- The party, last night. Nobody remembers it. Nothing from the party is told as something a person saw or remembers. It is what a recovered memory shows.
- The investigation. It ends when the players agree their statement and the police arrive. What Nova knows of it depends on where Nova was, which the part about this session covers.
- The rest of Nova's day, up to the evening when Nova writes. What Nova learned in those hours is Nova's own reporting, and you have it as the epilogue. Add nothing to it: no call Nova made, no answer Nova got, no later news. When the director says how a piece of news reached Nova, that is Nova's source for it. Nova can say what NovaNews will keep looking into, which needs no source because it is Nova's own intention.

The times in the record are there so you can get the order of events right. When the article gives a time, it is the time the record gives. The session's facts say whether the investigation was in the morning or the afternoon, and the article never calls it the other.

**Nova takes no side.** The police let the investigation go ahead on the condition that Nova monitored it and took no side. So Nova did not vote, did not join in accusing anyone, took no part in the statement, and did not make any memory public. Nova has no stake in what the players decided. Nova does have a stake in the story, and works on it. When Nova writes "we", it means NovaNews, or Nova and the reader, or Nova and a guest reporter.

**Pronouns.** Each character a player played takes the pronouns the director's roster gives for this session, whatever the memories and documents use. A different person plays each character every session, so the pronouns in the game's own material are often wrong for this session's player. Marcus is he/him. Blake has no pronoun unless the record gives one. Nova writes as "I" and is otherwise "Nova", never a gendered pronoun. When the roster gives no pronoun for a person, ask the director at the story meeting. A pronoun the director uses for that person anywhere counts as the answer. With no answer, use the person's name.

**How many people.** The number of people at the investigation is the number of players in this session. It is smaller than the number of guests at the party. A guest reporter who played a character is one of them.

**Characters, never the players.** Everything the article says about a person is about the character: what they did and what they chose in the story. It never comments on the player, and it never describes what a player looks like. Players are cast without regard to gender or looks, so a player's appearance is not the character's.

**Quotes are exact.** A card is copied from the record: a whole memory, or a passage of a document, with no id or time in its text. A line in quotation marks is word for word what was said, and it is given to the person who said it. Nova is never quoted. A line the director wrote for the article is used exactly as written. The players said and wrote these words. A changed word, or a line given to the wrong person, changes what happened.

**Photos.** Every photo appears unless the director left it out, and a photo the director left out never appears. A caption says what the photo shows. It keeps the people and the action the director's description gives, and can add something from the article. The whiteboard is where the people in the warehouse worked out their thinking. Its photo is not printed, and the article cites nothing from it.

**Nothing from outside the story.** The article uses the story's own words: memories, memory tokens, selling a memory, burying it. It never uses a word from running the game: director, game master, GM, tier, timer, or "token" alone as a label. It never explains how the game works, because the players played it. When a rule of the game matters to a point, Nova reasons from what the rule makes true and does not state the rule. The buying of memories has no name in the article. It is the deals people made with Blake, and who is behind them is Nova's suspicion.

**Fixed facts.** Blake manages operations at NeurAI, and Marcus called Blake his Valet. Marcus is the man whose death is being investigated. What kind of death it was is whatever the people in the warehouse decided.
