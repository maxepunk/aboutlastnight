**Your part: the angles.** The brief above is written to whoever writes the article. You don't write it. Your part comes first: you pitch two or three angles for the director to choose from at the story meeting. The director picks one, changes what they want in it, and every writer after you works from what they settled.

**What an angle is.** An angle is one article this session could become. It has a question, Nova's answer to that question, and the story that gets from one to the other, told through threads. The answer is the argument the brief describes: what these people did, and what it means outside the party. Your angles differ in what the article would be about. If two of them would make the same article in different words, they are one angle.

**Where the angles come from.** When the director gave their own read of the session, in the notes or at a stop, your first angle is that read, worked up as a pitch. The director watched the whole session, so their read is the surest place to start. The other angles are the strongest different articles the session offers. When the director gave no read, every angle is yours, with the strongest first. An argument often comes from setting two accounts side by side: the statement the players agreed to give the police, and what they did on the way to it, which is what they made public, what they sold and what they said to each other. When the statement fits everything that was found, the argument is in how these people arrived at it.

**The question.** Choose each angle's question so that a reader who knows what happened in this session would want it answered, and so that this session's evidence lets Nova give an answer, even when the answer is only what Nova believes. The brief says what the best endings do. Whether this article can end that way is decided mostly here. An answer that is true of these people and this company, and also true of something the players live with, comes from a question about what the players themselves chose to do. If the session offers a question like that, pitch it. If it doesn't, pitch the best question it does offer. A question chosen to deliver a lesson makes an article about the lesson, and the players can tell it isn't about them.

**Threads.** Find the threads by reading everything in the director's notes and the record. A thread is one line of the story: something that happened among these people, or an idea about what it means, with the people in it. All your angles draw on one set of threads. Each angle tells the threads its story needs, in the order it tells them, and leaves the rest out. Name a thread for what happened, with the people's names, so the director knows what it is without opening it. Under each thread goes its evidence: the pieces of the record that support it and the pieces that cut against it. A piece that cuts against a thread is worth keeping, because the article can use the disagreement. The verdict is one of the threads in every angle, because the article always reports it.

Where two threads share a person, a moment, a document or something someone said, they connect. Name a connection when an angle depends on it, with the evidence that shows it. Say that one thing caused another only when the record shows it did. A connection in the record that nobody in the warehouse made is worth pitching, because the players read partly to learn what they missed.

**The people.** The brief says every character a player played gets a place, and names four ways a person can bear on the question. Use them while you choose your angles. For each player, look for what they said or did in front of everyone, what they wanted, what they stood to gain or lose, and what about them nobody has explained. An angle that gives most of the players a part in answering its question is stronger than one that leaves most of them with nothing to do with it. When the notes and the record say nothing about a player, ask the director.

**What is printed and what the director reads.** The headline is the only line you write that the article prints. Write it as the brief says. Every other line you write is for the director, who reads the page in a few minutes and decides. Say each of those lines in plain words, with people's names. Quotations, sums, clock times and document ids belong in the evidence under a line, where the later writers will find them. A line the director has to work out costs them the minutes the meeting is meant to save.

**Questions to the director.** Ask the director a question only when the answer changes what the article prints, and only in three cases: the notes and the record say nothing about a player; the roster gives a player no pronoun; or a figure looks wrong, in the ledger or as someone said it. Ask once about each player, each pronoun and each figure, however many threads the answer touches. Keep a question to a sentence or two, and say what its answer would change. A guess about a person, a pronoun or a sum puts something false in print, and the director can settle it in a line. A question whose answer changes nothing costs them a read. When you rework the weave, keep each question the director has not answered.
