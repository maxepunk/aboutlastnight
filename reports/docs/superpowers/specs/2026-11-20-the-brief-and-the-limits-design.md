# Phase 18: the brief and the limits

The design for the second writing pass, which the director chose on 2026-11-19 to run before phase 3b. Agreed with the director on 2026-11-19 and 2026-11-20.

## Intent and the why

The draft the director opens at the desk should already read as journalism a player wants to finish.

Phase 17 aimed at this and ran live on 101726, 101826 and 111326. The director still rewrote each article. Their reading of their own edits (2026-11-19): the draft's lines were either clusters of words that mean nothing, or facts, quotes, times and names forced in from the input with no journalistic reason. They named a second fault, one that often survives their edits: the writer "crams most of the throughline in 'the story' section and then stuffs the remaining sections with threads that kinda seem to fit those sections best", which reads "meandering, repetitive, or disconnected".

What the three drafts show about the cause:

- **The writer prints what it is handed.** A rule's sentence became an article's sentence ("I name no killer", "went up anonymously"). A short line on the map became a line in the article ("Sarah puts her name to Riley's", "the room let it pass"). The evidence under the map's moves reaches the writer already written out, each piece with its quote, its time and the name on the turn-in. There were 76 to 81 pieces under 16 moves, for an article of about 1,450 words, and the drafts printed about two thirds of them.
- **The writer is told what happens at each move and never why the reader needs it.** So it has no way to decide what to leave out.
- **The writer reads about 8,500 words of numbered rules**, with 38 uses of "never" and 78 references from one rule to another. A model acts on every line it is given, and it writes in the manner it is addressed in.
- **The rules define each section by its kind of material**, which invites sorting. The map decides the sections, and its line for each is a topic of five to ten words ("Shows what the case set aside", "Turns the question on the brokers"). Nothing shows how one section leads to the next.

Phase 17 answered writing faults with more rules. The director (2026-11-19): that looks "more like patching over the symptoms instead of addressing the root causes of how and where the article writer is thinking incorrectly about how to approach their writing", which is "better resolved by clearer and more effective writing guidelines as opposed to hard and fast rules".

## What the director decided

- **A brief, with reasons, in place of craft rules.** The writers are taught how a reporter approaches this article: who reads it, what it argues, how a section and a paragraph are built, what goes in. Each part gives its reason. Hard rules stay only for the limits on what the article may claim.
- **Nothing the writer could print.** Phase 17 took example passages out because "the writer was frequently falling back to literally pulling text from the examples whether it made sense for this particular article or not". The three sessions show the writer lifts any finished sentence, so the brief and the limits hold no sample line, no "such as" aside and no sentence that would make sense inside an article.
- **The article has two jobs.** It is "a compelling piece of journalism 'in-fiction' while also serving the needs of the project as a game asset the players receive after role-playing as the characters within that fiction". Every line is written as journalism. Who the readers are decides what the article owes them and what it can leave unsaid because they already know it.
- **People do things.** "The 'room' didn't do anything." A person is the subject of each sentence about what a person did. This holds for the text the writers read as much as for the article, because the writers copy its manner.
- **Each section is the next step of one argument**, taken through that section's kind of material. The step decides what goes in a section. The sections also give every played character a way to be tied to the article's question, so every player can find themselves in it.
- **The best endings do two things.** They bring the article's question "to its natural and satisfying conclusion", and they "explore a theme from the players' real world through the fiction of the game". The second "is not always possible and somewhat depends on player choices and playstyle for any given session".
- **The director is not in the story.** What the director's notes say happened in the warehouse is, in the story, what Nova saw and heard on site, or "tips and messages" Nova received from the warehouse "along with the tokens that were turned in". The limits on what Nova knows are worded by Nova's sources.
- **The headline and the line under it are taught as journalism**: what each has to do for a reader, with reasons, and no formula for their wording.
- **People are more than one thing.** "Player choices are significant regardless of whether they cast a CHARACTER in a positive and negative light", and a character who played for money and power and also helped someone gets both told, each framed "from the lens of the fiction of the article".
- **A theory the players debated is told when the argument needs it.** It is no longer always reported ("Drop 'always'").
- **The article does not announce where Nova was.** "The players already know."
- **The notes on what was taken out never reach a prompt** (2026-11-20).

The brief's eighteen parts and the reworded world, limits and two parts on where Nova was are settled word for word (2026-11-20, "looks fine"). They are appendix A, and this phase puts them in front of the writers and the judges unchanged.

## Goals, non-goals, anti-goals

**Goals.**
- The unsteered draft makes one argument that runs through every section, and each section leaves the reader wanting the next.
- Every played character is in the article through something that bears on its question.
- What the article prints is there because the reader needs it, in sentences the writer wrote for the reader.
- The article reads as a report from inside the world. The game's log, the director and the writer's own instructions stay off the page.

**Non-goals.**
- The stops' pages, beyond one line on the map (section 4).
- The evidence path (phase 3b) and the change review (phase 4b, piece 2).
- A judge or a check on the writing. The judges stay truth-only.
- Example passages in any prompt.
- How much evidence sits under each move, and how each piece is worded. If the next session's draft still prints the evidence wholesale, that is the next thing to change (see "What to watch").

**Anti-goals.** The phase fails, even with every test green, if it produces:
- the brief's own sentences in an article;
- a new form the writer fills, such as the question stated in the same place in the same words every session, or a step announced at the top of each section;
- an ending that states a moral about the real world;
- a longer set of rules under a new name;
- a draft that is only good when the director steered early.

## Constraints

- **The settled text goes in word for word.** The five files of appendix A are the rule files. One sentence differs from what the director read (section 1). A test pins each file by its hash, so any later change is deliberate and carries its reason.
- **The notes stay out.** Appendix B is for people. Nothing in it goes into a rule file, a task, a label or a schema.
- **No example prose.** No text a model reads holds a sample line, a "such as" aside or a sentence from any session.
- **The stops keep their shapes.** No field is added to or removed from the weave, the map or the article, so old sessions still open and nothing stored has to be migrated.
- **The director's edits stay final.** Nothing here touches what holds a rework to them.
- **No gate makes a live model call, and no old session is rerun live.** The test is the director's next new session, as they confirmed on 2026-11-20.
- **Every line a model reads that this phase words anew is the director's to approve before the plan**, as a rule-text read, the step every phase since 3 has used.

**Where each sits in a prompt.** The system prompt opens as it does today: who the writer is, the part about this session, the world, the limits. That part reaches the prompt in a tag named `this-session`, whichever file it came from, so the three references point at something the reader can find.

**Size.** The article writer reads about 8,500 words of rules today. It will read about 5,200: the world, the limits, the part about this session, the brief and its own part.

### 3. Each writer's own part

The brief is written to whoever writes the article. Two of its three readers do not write it: one pitches its angles and one plans it. So each writer gets a short text of its own, read right after the brief, that says which of the brief's work is theirs, where to find what was settled before them, and the working instructions of their own stop. It is written in the brief's manner: plain prose with reasons, no numbered rules, no examples, a person as the subject of what a person does.

What each holds, to be worded for the rule-text read:

**The weave writer** pitches two or three angles at the story meeting.
- An angle is one article this session could become: its question, Nova's answer, its headline, the story in two or three sentences, why it lands with the players, and the threads it tells. Angles differ in what the article would be about.
- When the director gave their own read, the first angle is that read.
- The choice of question is where the ending is decided. A question whose answer, said only about these people and this company, is also true of something the players live with gives the ending its second job. A question chosen to deliver a lesson does not.
- Each person is looked at through the four ways the brief names. When the record says nothing about a player, that is a question for the director.
- Threads are named in plain words with their people, each with the evidence that supports it and the evidence that cuts against it. The verdict is always one of them. A link in the record that nobody in the warehouse made is material.
- The headline is the one printed line this writer writes. Every other line is for the director to read in minutes: plain words, with the quotations, figures and times left in the evidence underneath.
- The three cases for a question to the director, as today.

**The map writer** plans the article from the angle the director settled.
- The steps of the argument are this writer's work, and each section's line on the map is its step (section 4).
- Every played character is placed on a move through one of the four ways. A player who cannot be tied to the question is named in the line at the top of the map. No tie is made up.
- The evidence under a move is what the article writer may draw on to take that step, chosen because the step needs it.
- The line under the headline and the headings are printed lines, written as the brief says. The headline is the director's and is carried as it is. Every other line is for the director.
- The working instructions as today: fitting in the director's changes from the meeting and adding no thread; the cards; what was considered and left out; photos beside their moves; the length, about 1,500 words at most, set here by striking a move when the map runs long.

**The article writer** writes the article.
- The steps, the order and each person's place were settled on the map, as the director left it. This writer writes every move on it and no other, and what the director struck stays out.
- The headline, the line under it and the headings are printed as written. Every other sentence is this writer's own.
- The working instructions as today: the cards, the sidebar and the quote blocks with their counts; the map's expected length, where a thin session makes a shorter article; the house style on dashes.

Each writer's task block in code (the few lines that give this call's bounds and fields) stays, with its pointers into the craft rules replaced by pointers to these texts by name.

### 4. The map shows the steps

Each section on the map already carries one line, "Job: what the section does to the question". It becomes the section's step: one plain sentence saying what the reader learns there about the question and what that leaves them wanting to know. Read top to bottom, the five or six lines are the argument, in order, before any move is opened. This is where the director can see, and change, how the story runs through the sections.

### 5. The weave carries the question and its answer

An angle already has the fields the brief needs: its question, and "where it ends up", which is Nova's answer. No field changes. Their descriptions say so in the brief's words.

The weave writer is told today to examine each thread through three lenses: the money, what people did, and who was harmed. They descend from three retired specialist calls. The director's own use of "lenses" (091826: "the story being told throughout the entire article using the different lenses") is about the sections. So the three lenses give way to the brief's four ways a person can bear on the question, which are the sections: what they said or did in front of everyone, what they wanted, what they stood to gain or lose, and what about them nobody has explained. The weave then finds its people along the same lines the map lays them on.

The limits say "made public", "turned in" and "sold". The record the same writer reads says "exposed", "exposure", "named" and "anonymous", and the drafts printed those ("an exposure under her own name", "went up anonymously"). The director's check on the brief was to use the words the writer's other inputs use for the same thing. So the inputs change to the limits' words.

| Today, in what the writers read | After |
|---|---|
| an exposed memory | a memory that was made public |
| an exposure | a memory being turned in |
| the exposer; "named: Taylor"; "anonymous" | the person who turned it in; "name given: Taylor"; "no name given" |
| a buried memory | a sold memory. The article may still say "buried", which is the story's word |
| the room did, chose, debated, dropped | the person or people who did it, or "everyone in the warehouse" when it was everyone |
| the room's verdict | the verdict, or the statement the players agreed |
| the reporting-mode block | the part about this session |
| T1, C16 and the like | the name of the part (section 7) |

What is reworded: each writer's task block and field list; the descriptions in the weave's, the map's and the questions' schemas; the record's opening line and its timeline; the labels over the director's notes, the epilogue, the accusation and the whiteboard; the session's facts; the messages a rework reads from the weave's, the map's and the article's checks; the judges' questions. The article's schema also still shows the writer fields it is told to leave out, one of them with stale lines about "voice influences" and "anti-patterns". The schema the writer is shown will describe only the fields it writes.

What is left alone: the line that tells each writer who it is, including "the players who were in the room", which the director chose in phase 17; field names and stored data; the prompts of calls that write nothing the article prints (sorting the evidence, describing photos, reading the notes).

### 7. No rule numbers where a writer reads

The writers' text names a part by its name: "as 'Everyone gets a place' says". No number appears in anything a writer reads, and nothing points from one limit to another.

The judges keep T1 to T15 as codes on their findings. Code reads the code to tell which check a finding belongs to and whether it falls in the director's own text, and stored sessions hold findings in that form. The limits file carries no codes. The judge's own instructions list the fifteen limits by code and name, in the file's order, and a test holds that list equal to the names in `limits.md`. The desk already strips the code before the director sees a finding, so the page does not change.

### 8. The judges and the automatic checks follow the limits

Each judge question is reworded from the limit it checks. Three change in what they ask:

- **The verdict.** The judge no longer checks that every debated theory is reported, or that a struck theory stays out. It checks that the verdict is told as the account the players agreed, that Nova does not call it right or wrong, and that a verdict blaming nobody is not reported as blaming someone. A move the director struck still stays out of the article, by the article writer's own part, and the director's desk edits are held by code as before.
- **Where Nova was.** The check that counts how often an article says Nova was not there keeps its count and stays a note to the director. Its line now says what the limit says: the players know, and it comes up only when something Nova tells them depends on it.
- **A claim that Nova saw something from outside.** The fix it gives a rework now says what the limit says: Nova was told and did not see, and names who told Nova only when the notes say.

The checks' own logic and numbers do not change: the words from running the game, the 1,500-word aim and the 1,800-word flag, the head count, the pronouns, the dash, the three to five cards.

## How the work runs

1. The director read this spec and settled its three open points (2026-11-20).
2. The rule-text read: the three writers' own parts in full, and each reworded task block, description, label, judge question and check message, old beside new. Approved before the plan.
3. The plan, in the project's brief-per-slice format. Slices run in their own worktrees and are integrated into one phase branch.
4. The gate: the full suite; every call's prompt rendered with no model call and diffed against `main`, each change one a brief names; every writer's render scanned for rule numbers and for the sentences of appendix B; the article writer's whole prompt read top to bottom as the writer would meet it. Rendering a real session needs a copy of the database, which needs the director's go-ahead, as in phase 17.
5. The merge to `main` on the director's yes, with no session paused at the input review or a later stop. The server then needs a restart to read the new files.

## Appendix B: notes for people, never for a prompt

**Decided alongside the brief**
- A theory the players debated is told when a step of the argument needs it. It is no longer always reported.
- "A scene, then the evidence that confirms or complicates it" is not a separate instruction. Its purpose is in the second paragraph on Nova.
- The article does not announce where Nova was. The players know.

**What is no longer in the limits, and why**
- The opening paragraph on what the article is for. The brief says it.
- Every theory the players debated gets a line. Dropped by the director on 2026-11-20.
- How Nova words a suspicion or an opinion. The brief's part on Nova says it. The limit keeps only that such a thing is never stated as fact.
- "Nova says so once, early" about being outside. The players know.
- "The room's events are told as scenes." The brief says how to tell what happened.
- The asides that began "such as". The drafts printed them.
- "Exposed", "exposure" and "exposer". The limits say "made public" and "turned in", because the drafts printed the old words as the game's log read aloud.
- "The room" as the one who did something. People did each thing.
- References from one rule to another by number.
