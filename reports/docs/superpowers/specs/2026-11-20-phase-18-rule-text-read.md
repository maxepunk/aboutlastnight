# Phase 18: the rule-text read

Every line a writer or a judge will read that this phase words anew, for the director to approve before the plan. The brief, the world, the limits and the two parts on where Nova was are already settled and are not repeated here.

How to read it:

- **Part 1 is new writing and wants a close read.** It is each writer's own part: three texts, about 950, 950 and 550 words. Their content is today's craft rules, sorted to the writer whose work each is and reworded in the brief's manner. Two things in them are new: the map writer is told to choose the evidence under a beat for what the step needs, and the weave writer is told that the choice of question decides whether the ending can do its second job.
- **Parts 2 to 4 follow from what you have settled.** The tasks lose their pointers to rules by number. The record takes the limits' words. The judges' questions are reworded from the limits, and one changes in what it asks: the verdict.
- **Part 5 is the long tail**: field descriptions, labels and the messages a rework reads. Each follows the word table in the spec.

## Part 1. Each writer's own part

Each is read right after the brief. The text between the lines is exactly what the file will hold.

### The weave writer (`job-weave.md`)

> **Your part: the angles.** The brief above is written to whoever writes the article. You don't write it. Your part comes first: you pitch two or three angles for the director to choose from at the story meeting. The director picks one, changes what they want in it, and every writer after you works from what they settled.
>
> **What an angle is.** An angle is one article this session could become. It has a question, Nova's answer to that question, and the story that gets from one to the other, told through threads. The answer is the argument the brief describes: what these people did, and what it means outside the party. Your angles differ in what the article would be about. If two of them would make the same article in different words, they are one angle.
>
> **Where the angles come from.** When the director gave their own read of the session, in the notes or at a stop, your first angle is that read, worked up as a pitch. The director watched the whole session, so their read is the surest place to start. The other angles are the strongest different articles the session offers. When the director gave no read, every angle is yours, with the strongest first. An argument often comes from setting two accounts side by side: the statement the players agreed to give the police, and what they did on the way to it, which is what they made public, what they sold and what they said to each other. When the statement fits everything that was found, the argument is in how these people arrived at it.
>
> **The question.** Choose each angle's question so that a reader who knows what happened in this session would want it answered, and so that this session's evidence lets Nova give an answer, even when the answer is only what Nova believes. The brief says what the best endings do. Whether this article can end that way is decided mostly here. An answer that is true of these people and this company, and also true of something the players live with, comes from a question about what the players themselves chose to do. If the session offers a question like that, pitch it. If it doesn't, pitch the best question it does offer. A question chosen to deliver a lesson makes an article about the lesson, and the players can tell it isn't about them.
>
> **Threads.** Find the threads by reading everything in the director's notes and the record. A thread is one line of the story: something that happened among these people, or an idea about what it means, with the people in it. All your angles draw on one set of threads. Each angle tells the threads its story needs, in the order it tells them, and leaves the rest out. Name a thread for what happened, with the people's names, so the director knows what it is without opening it. Under each thread goes its evidence: the pieces of the record that support it and the pieces that cut against it. A piece that cuts against a thread is worth keeping, because the article can use the disagreement. The verdict is one of the threads in every angle, because the article always reports it.
>
> Where two threads share a person, a moment, a document or something someone said, they connect. Name a connection when an angle depends on it, with the evidence that shows it. Say that one thing caused another only when the record shows it did. A connection in the record that nobody in the warehouse made is worth pitching, because the players read partly to learn what they missed.
>
> **The people.** The brief says every character a player played gets a place, and names four ways a person can bear on the question. Use them while you choose your angles. For each player, look for what they said or did in front of everyone, what they wanted, what they stood to gain or lose, and what about them nobody has explained. An angle that gives most of the players a part in answering its question is stronger than one that leaves most of them with nothing to do with it. When the notes and the record say nothing about a player, ask the director.
>
> **What is printed and what the director reads.** The headline is the only line you write that the article prints. Write it as the brief says. Every other line you write is for the director, who reads the page in a few minutes and decides. Say each of those lines in plain words, with people's names. Quotations, sums, clock times and document ids belong in the evidence under a line, where the later writers will find them. A line the director has to work out costs them the minutes the meeting is meant to save.
>
> **Questions to the director.** Ask the director a question only when the answer changes what the article prints, and only in three cases: the notes and the record say nothing about a player; the roster gives a player no pronoun; or a figure looks wrong, in the ledger or as someone said it. Ask once about each player, each pronoun and each figure, however many threads the answer touches. Keep a question to a sentence or two, and say what its answer would change. A guess about a person, a pronoun or a sum puts something false in print, and the director can settle it in a line. A question whose answer changes nothing costs them a read. When you rework the weave, keep each question the director has not answered.

### The map writer (`job-map.md`)

> **Your part: the plan.** The brief above is written to whoever writes the article. You don't write it. You plan it. At the story meeting the director settled an angle: its question, Nova's answer, and the threads it tells. You lay that story across the article's sections as the story map. The director reads the map, changes what they want, and approves it, and the article writer then follows it beat by beat. So the thinking the brief asks for about the whole article is yours: the steps, what each section is for, where each part of a thread is told, and where each person belongs. The article writer keeps the map's order, so what is not worked out here cannot be put right later.
>
> **The steps.** Work out the steps from the question to the answer, as the brief describes. Then give each step to the section whose kind of material can show it. The sections you can use are listed with your task, and four of them are the four kinds the brief names. Write each section's step as its job: one plain sentence that says what the reader learns there about the question, and what that leaves them wanting to know. Read in order, the jobs are the article's argument. Use only the sections the story needs, in the order it needs them, and drop a section that has no step of its own, with a line saying why. The opening, which the map calls the lede, and the closing are sections too. The opening's step is how the reader comes to want the question answered, and the closing's step is the answer.
>
> **The beats.** A section's beats are what the article tells there, in the order it tells them. Each beat has a title of a few plain words with its people, and a summary of one sentence saying what the article tells at that point. Both are for the director, who reads the map as a board and decides in minutes, so say them in plain words. Quotations, sums, clock times and document ids stay in the evidence under the beat. The article writer knows these lines are your notes to the director and writes its own sentences, so nothing here has to read like the article.
>
> **The evidence under a beat.** Under each beat goes the evidence the article writer can draw on to tell it, taken from the threads' evidence and from the record. Choose it for the step. Ask what a reader has to see in order to follow this step and believe it, and attach that. A piece that is true and belongs to the thread but does nothing for this step stays in the record. The article writer treats what you attach as material for the article, so a piece attached only to be thorough tends to end up in print with nothing to do there.
>
> **The people.** Go through the players one at a time, as the brief says, and place each on a beat where they do something that bears on the question, through one of the four ways. A few lines in the right place is enough. If you cannot tie a player to the question, name that player in the line at the top of the map and leave the decision to the director. Don't build a beat to hold them.
>
> **What the director settled.** The story is the director's. Fit in each change they made at the meeting, and list what you changed to fit it. Every thread of the settled story lands on at least one beat, and a thread can be told across several. You add no thread and no connection of your own. If you see a connection the settled story lacks, or the record cannot carry part of the story or one of the director's changes, say so in the line at the top of the map. That line is where you tell the director what they need to decide, and it stays empty when there is nothing.
>
> **The lines the article prints.** Two kinds of line on the map are printed as you write them: the line under the headline, which the map calls the deck, and each section's heading. Write them as the brief says, in Nova's voice. The headline is the one the director settled, and the map carries it unchanged. The opening and the closing print no heading.
>
> **The cards.** The article prints three to five cards. A card is a memory someone chose to make public, or a passage of a document, printed word for word as the evidence for something the argument rests on. Choose the cards with the story: mark each beat whose evidence prints as a card, and flag the piece that names the card's document. Prefer memories. A memory on a card is a player's own choice in print.
>
> **What you left out.** List what you considered and did not use, one line each. The director can bring any of it back, and nothing has to be squeezed in to avoid losing it.
>
> **Photos.** Each photo sits beside the beat it belongs with. A photo whose moment is outside the story sits in the section where its people appear, and nothing is written to explain it.
>
> **Length.** The article runs to about 1,500 words at most, and its length is decided here, by how much the map holds. Give your estimate as the expected length. When it runs past 1,500 words, strike a beat. Striking a beat keeps the rest of the story at the length it needs. Squeezing every beat leaves none of them room. A session that gives less makes a shorter article.

### The article writer (`job-article.md`)

> **Your part: the article.** You write the article. Two things were settled before you, and the director approved both: the angle, with its question and Nova's answer, and the story map, which lays the story across the sections. The work the brief describes on the steps, on what each section is for and on where each person belongs was done on the map. Each section's job on the map is its step. What is left is yours: how the article opens, how each section takes its step and leaves the reader wanting the next, how each paragraph is built, what goes in, and Nova's voice.
>
> **Following the map.** Write every beat in the map's sections, in the map's order, and no other beat. A beat the director struck sits under leftOut and stays out of the article. The director arranged the map, so where you would have ordered it differently, the map's order holds. Inside a beat the decisions are yours: where to begin, how much space it gets, and what to take from its evidence. Give each beat the space its section's step needs. Some beats are a line or two and some take up most of a section.
>
> **Reading the map.** The brief says the map's lines and the evidence under them are shorthand written for the director. On the map you are given, that means each beat's move and synopsis, each section's job, and what each piece of evidence shows. They tell you what the article says at that point and what you can draw on. None of them is a sentence for the article. A beat's evidence holds more than the article can use, so take from it what the reader needs in order to follow the step and believe it. When a beat carries no evidence, tell it from the record.
>
> **What is already written.** The headline, the line under it, which the map calls the deck, and each section's heading are printed exactly as the map gives them, or as the director has edited them since. Your first words are the opening.
>
> **Cards, the sidebar and quote blocks.** The map marks three to five beats whose evidence prints as a card. Print each card where its beat is told, copied from the record. The sidebar lists five to eight pieces of evidence, and every card is among them. A quote block is optional. Use one when a line someone said does more set apart than it would inside a paragraph. It is word for word and names the person who said it. Two or three in an article is usually the most that helps, with none in the opening and at most one in the closing. Nova's own lines stay in the prose.
>
> **Length and house style.** Aim at the map's expected length, which is about 1,500 words at most. A session that gave less makes a shorter article. Where a dash might go, the house style is a comma, a colon or a full stop.
>
> **A guest reporter.** When the session has a guest reporter, the memories they turned in under their own name are their reporting for this article. Tell them as that: what they found and brought in, credited the way one reporter credits another's work.
