# The rule set

The single source for phase 3, "One rule set". Phase 3 builds the model-facing rules from this document. Every writer and judge then reads them: the arc calls, the interweaving call, the outline and article writers, their reworkers and the three judges. Phase 3 also moves into code what code can guarantee, and removes the rules listed under "What goes".

This document holds the director's decisions of 2026-09-25 to 2026-10-01:
- the rulings sheet (`docs/superpowers/specs/2026-09-25-rule-rulings.md`, cited by entry id, such as EX1);
- the phase 3 prep grill that followed, and the review of the phase 3 plan, whose decisions are listed in the appendix (cited by number, such as F2 or R3).

Where the rulings sheet and this document differ, this document wins. The vocabulary is `CONTEXT.md`'s.

## 1. What the article is for

The game's own how-to-play page makes the players a promise: "a personalized investigative report on the Nova News blog, showing how your choices shaped the official story. You'll catch pieces of the narrative you may have missed during your session." The article keeps that promise for the players who were in the room. They read it to:
- see themselves, through their characters' choices, their lines and their moments of grace;
- catch what they missed: connections in the record the room never made;
- see how their choices shaped the official story: what they exposed, what they sold, what they argued, and what the verdict served.

They have to read it to the end, so it runs to about 1,500 words (F2). The article is real investigative journalism in Nova's first person, and the fiction stays whole.

## 2. The world

Every rule below uses these facts. They come from the how-to-play page and the director's confirmations (rulings sheet, "Game facts"; T3).

- **The party** happened last night. Marcus Blackwood drugged his guests and extracted their memories into memory tokens. The characters cannot remember the party; everyone, Nova included, meets it only through recovered memories.
- **The investigation** is the game itself, this morning, in the warehouse. Players unlock memory tokens, scan them and decide what each one becomes:
  - trade: give it, swap it, use it as leverage, or return it to its owner;
  - expose: turn it in to Nova; its summary goes up on the Evidence Board for everyone to see;
  - bury: sell it to be erased for ever; NeurAI's board pays the seller into an account the seller names, and pays more the more sensitive the memory.
- **The Valet**, Blake, introduces himself with "I manage operations at NeurAI"; Marcus would usually call him his Valet (T3). Blake moves about the room and pulls players into quiet corners to trade; some sales happen openly.
- **The ledger** records every transaction with its time and amount: each sale into an account, the first-burial bonus, each transfer between accounts. It never shows which memory was sold. The room sees only the running balances.
- **An account** is wherever a seller sends the money, under any name, including another character's. A shell can be shared.
- **The evidence log** records each exposure with its time and the name on the turn-in: "NovaNews (Anonymous)" or a name the player chose to put on it.
- **The group statement** is the verdict: the official story. When the recovery window closes, sales end, the police are on their way, and the room must agree one witness statement. It can cite only what is on the board, it leaves out whatever the room chooses, and it is a version every character can live with. So every choice the players made shapes it, and a buried memory can never be cited in it.
- **Nova** is an independent journalist who had been investigating Marcus. Fremont PD required Nova to monitor the investigation as an uninterested third party: the condition of Blake's deal to delay sending officers while the room investigates, agrees its statement and leaves (T3). Exposed memories are turned in to Nova. On site Nova is in the warehouse; remote, Nova monitors from outside and the room's events reach Nova from people in it. Nova takes no part in the statement.
- **Nova's day** follows the investigation: Nova writes that evening, with the follow-up reporting of the afternoon and evening. The director writes that follow-up into the notes as the epilogue.
- **There is no right answer.** The session has no answer key. The room authors the story through its choices.

### 3a. How a memory moves through the game and the data

By the end of the game every memory token has met one fate, and each fate leaves a different trace.

| Fate | What the room saw | What Nova has | What the pipeline's data holds | What the article can do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exposed | Its summary on the Evidence Board | The full memory | The memory's text; an evidence-log entry with the time and the name on the turn-in | Print it whole on a card, giving the room the full memory it saw only as a summary. Name its owner. Credit the exposer only if named (T6). Place it on the timeline |
| Buried | A balance changing on the live ledger | A ledger line: time, amount, account | The memory's text and owner, and which sale erased it | Report the sale's time, size and account; never its content or owner, except as talk the director's notes record, with the speaker named (T3) |
| Traded, held or returned | Nothing, unless people talked | Nothing, unless the director's notes record it | The memory's text, with no session entry | Nothing as evidence; talk the notes record, with the speaker named |
| Never found | Nothing | Nothing | The memory's text | Nothing. A character's absence from the board is a fact about the board, never a sign of a burial (BU1) |

Three relationships follow.
- **The ledger also holds money tied to no memory:** the first-burial bonus, and transfers between accounts.
- **The evidence log and the ledger share the game's clock.** Together they are the morning's timeline: what was going public set against what was being erased (section 6).
- **The data always knows more than Nova.** The pipeline holds every memory's text, the unfound and the buried included, and the session report says which memory each sale erased. Nova's ledger does not. So code withholds it, and a buried memory reaches the writers only as its ledger line, which phase 2 already enforces. A writer never has to hold back what it never sees.

## 4. Truth rules

Truth rules keep the article true to the record and to how the game works. Each is stated with its reason, so a writer can decide the edge cases. Every writer and every judge reads all of them. "Checked by" says what enforces the rule:
- "code" means a programmatic check;
- "judge" means the judge treats a breach as must-fix: the draft goes back for an automatic rework, within the automated budget, and the finding names the sentence and the record it contradicts. A breach is a definite error with the draft; flagging it to the director would only hand the director the same fix (R2). The claim check (phase 7) later reads every claim against the record.

### T1. Evidence decides how a claim is written

- What the record backs (an exposed memory, a document, the ledger, the evidence log, a photo), Nova states, and shows when it carries the thesis.
- What happened or was said in the room, which the director's notes record, Nova reports as seen or heard on site, or as reported to Nova remotely, with the speaker named.
- What Nova knows or suspects but cannot back, Nova presents as a reading, an unproven claim or a question. Examples: background from earlier reporting on Marcus, or talk in the room about a memory that was later sold.

Nothing is invented.
- *Reason.* The players know what happened, and a false detail about their character breaks the gift. Receipts versus allegations is how Nova earns belief.
- *Source.* DS principle 4; TH1; D4; T1.
- *Checked by.* Judge; the claim check (phase 7).

### T2. The verdict is the group's negotiated official story

The article reports the verdict as the official story. It then shows how the players' choices made it: what the statement cited and left out, the possibilities that surfaced and were dropped, and whose interests it served. It never claims to know what really happened to Marcus, and it never grades the verdict against a hidden answer. A verdict with no culprit (an overdose, an accident) names no one. The alternative theories the room debated are always reported.
- *Reason.* The statement is a story everyone could live with, built under the clock from what was on the board. Showing how it was made is the article's promise.
- *Source.* TH5; D1, D1a, D1b, D9; the how-to-play page.
- *Checked by.* Judge.

### T3. Buried memories never appear as evidence

No sentence presents what a buried memory said, or whose it was, as fact. If players talked about a memory in the room and the director's notes record it, Nova reports what was said, with the speaker named. When a memory was sold, for how much and into which account are all reportable. A sale's size may speak to what was at stake, since the board pays more the more sensitive the memory (R10).
- *Reason.* The game erased those memories, and Nova's ledger cannot show what was sold.
- *Source.* BU3; T1.
- *Checked by.* Judge.

### T4. An account is the seller's chosen destination and name

The name is a message the seller chose: a joke, a borrowed identity, the seller's own name, or a frame. It is never proof of who holds the account, and a shell can be shared. Nova reads the name for what it claims, the timing for when, and the director's notes for who:
- A person is tied to an account only by what the director saw or by an open claim, and posed as Nova's question unless confirmed.
- A sale made openly in front of the room is a fact.
- A count-level reading that names no one is Nova's to make.

Totals show where the morning's money went. A transfer is a deal between players.
- *Reason.* Anyone can open an account in any name, and the live ledger does not mark whose an account is.
- *Source.* AC1, AC2, AC3; T2.
- *Checked by.* Judge.

### T5. The board pays the seller

Money runs from NeurAI's board to the seller's chosen account. The ledger's totals are the figures, and the writer computes nothing. The first-burial bonus and transfers are reportable money. So are each account's total, every sale's time and size, how many sales an account took, and the morning's bursts and lulls ("The Ember account took four sales between 10:02 and 10:14, $2 million in all"), and counts of exposed memories, overall and per owner ("three were Alex's") (BU2). The ledger records only what was paid to bury memories this morning, and says nothing about anyone's other wealth (Marcus's fortune, an inheritance, an investment). An entry that looks like a mistake goes to the director as a question (C15), not into print.
- *Reason.* Money running the wrong way is the most common factual slip, and reading the ledger as proof of other wealth is a category error.
- *Source.* BU2, BU5; the director's notes on money pools; R1.
- *Checked by.* Code for totals; judge for direction.

### T6. Exposers are anonymous unless named

An exposure is anonymous unless the evidence log carries a name, which is an honest attribution, or the director's notes record who turned the memory in. A memory's owner is never assumed to be its exposer. A card's citation never names the exposer.
- *Reason.* At turn-in the player chooses whether to go on the record, and anonymity protects the source, even from an on-site Nova.
- *Source.* EX1; D6; D16a.
- *Checked by.* Judge.

### T7. The stages and the clock

- **The party** exists only as memories, never as something witnessed.
- **The investigation** is told as the reporting mode allows. It ends in the group statement as the police come.
- **Nova's day** comes from the epilogue alone. The writer adds no follow-up fact or outreach result the epilogue does not give. All follow-up is Nova's own reporting, and a channel the director names (a leaked email, an anonymous tip) is Nova's source for that item.

Logged times appear on the morning clock (code, section 6). "Tonight" and "this afternoon" belong only to Nova's day.
- *Reason.* The players lived the investigation, met the party only through memories, and know nothing of the aftermath until Nova reports it.
- *Source.* TL1, TL2; D2, D3; stages (round 5).
- *Checked by.* Code for the clock; judge for the stages.

### T8. Nova is the uninterested third party

Nova never votes, accuses or exposes, and is never one of the room. On site, Nova saw and heard the investigation. Remote, the investigation reached Nova from people in the room; Nova shows this through attribution and states the absence at most once. Nova's "we" is NovaNews, Nova and the reader, or Nova and a guest reporter; on site it may include the room only for being there.
- *Reason.* The police deal made Nova a neutral monitor, and the reporting mode sets what Nova could witness.
- *Source.* PR1 to PR4; T3.
- *Checked by.* Code for presence and vote claims, already in the fact check; judge.

### T9. Pronouns

Players take the pronouns on the director's roster; the pronoun set at the roster stop is the director's choice, they/them included (R3). Marcus is he/him. Blake has none invented. Nova is never referred to with gendered pronouns: Nova writes in the first person and is otherwise "Nova". A player who reaches the writers with no roster pronoun at all is raised as a question (C15), never guessed.
- *Reason.* The same character is played by different people, so canon pronouns are wrong for players. Pronoun errors were among the most frequent fixes.
- *Source.* PE1; T4.
- *Checked by.* Code for Marcus, Blake and Nova; judge for players.

### T10. The head count

When the article says how many people were in the room, it counts the players at the investigation, a subset of the party's guests. A guest reporter who shares the byline and plays a character is one of them.
- *Source.* D16b.
- *Checked by.* Code.

### T11. Characters, not players

Commentary and judgement land on the characters' choices, never on the players themselves. A player's appearance is never described.
- *Reason.* The players read about their own characters. Players are cast across genders, and a look is not a character.
- *Source.* F4; D16g.
- *Checked by.* Judge.

### T12. Words are exact

- A card copies the record: a memory whole, a document's passage. Its text carries no id or timestamp.
- A quoted line is word for word and names its real speaker, never "Nova".
- The director's lines, when used, are exact.
- *Reason.* The players wrote and said these words. A misquote, or a line in the wrong mouth, changes the story.
- *Source.* CA1, CA3, CA4; D9.
- *Checked by.* Code for cards (the fact check); judge for quotes.

### T13. Photos and the whiteboard

Every photo the director has not excluded appears; an excluded photo never does. A caption says what the photo shows and keeps the subject and action of the director's description; it may add context from the article. The whiteboard photo is never placed, and the whiteboard is never cited.
- *Reason.* The players are in the photos, and the whiteboard is the room's working notes, not a source.
- *Source.* PH1, PH2, PH3; D10, D16c, F3; R1.
- *Checked by.* Judge; caption checks in phase 9.

### T14. The fiction stays whole

No production words appear in print: director, GM, game master, tier, timer, or a bare "token" or "tokens" as a system label. "Memory token", "bury", "sold" and "memories" are the fiction's own words. There is no "Detective" receiving memories, no "Valet station" and no "Black Market" as a named institution: memories go to Nova, Blake moves about the room, and the market is the deals made with Blake.
- *Source.* BU2, VO5; the game facts; the director's voice notes ("the Black Market" becomes the deals made with Blake).
- *Checked by.* Code for production words; judge.

### T15. Canon

Blake manages operations at NeurAI, and Marcus called Blake his Valet. Marcus is the man whose death the room investigates. What his death was is whatever the room decided (T2).
- *Source.* D7; D1.

## 5. Craft guidance

Craft guidance is what makes the article worth reading to the end. It is taught as what to do and why, never as quotas or templates. The writers read it. A new judge finding on craft is should-consider. The judges' existing must-fix checks keep their status, each reworded to name the item it scores, until phase 7 calibrates them (R2). Each item comes from the director's words or the direction of the director's edits (round 4, round 6).

### C1. One thesis, defined before writing, and everything serves it

When the director's notes give a thesis, the writer runs with it. Otherwise the writer finds the most interesting journalistic angle on what happened. That is usually the gap between the verdict and what happened leading up to it, and what the gap shows about the group's biases. When the verdict fits everything the room found, it is the angle the players explored together.
- *Why.* It makes the article about this room, not a recap.
- *Source.* F1; D1a, D1b; the director's 091826 note that the thesis was not "properly defined" or "connected to the article contents".

### C2. Every section is an essential part of one narrative

Every section is a movement of the same story, carrying the threads forward from its own angle: what happened, the money, the players, what is missing. Nothing is front-loaded into THE STORY, and no section is a checklist to plug leftover material into. Not every arc appears in every section. The thesis runs through every section without being said twice, and the narrative builds to it by the end (C16). The thesis also decides which sections exist, their order and their headings: open with the verdict when the verdict is the story, and drop a section whose job is done elsewhere.
- *Why.* A compelling journalistic narrative that culminates in a strong thesis, read to the end.
- *Source.* TH4; the director's 091826 note on the front-loaded story "instead of the story being told throughout the entire article using the different lenses"; the director's thesis-driven note; R6.

### C3. No right answer

The article never settles what really happened and never grades the verdict (T2). A pattern in the record that the room did not take up is framed as that, never as the solution.
- *Source.* TH5; the director's 091826 headline fix.

### C4. About 1,500 words, and every paragraph and card pulls the reader on

Cut recaps, scaffolding, flourishes, aphorisms and lines that announce what is coming. The length forces choices: the thesis decides what earns space. What Nova cannot see gets at most one line, then the story. No em-dashes, a house rule; code checks for them (DT1; the director's em-dash note). Code also flags the narrator's prose (headline, deck and paragraphs) above 1,800 words, as an advisory (R4).
- *Why.* Players stop reading long articles and flat paragraphs.
- *Source.* TH3; F2; BU4; the director's 091826 note ("each paragraph and evidence card should be designed to make the reader want to continue reading the next"); the director's cuts on 091826 and 071826; R1, R4.

### C5. The headline is a short hook; the deck carries the tension; the lede opens on a moment

The headline carries one idea, with a name in it, and does not spend the article's strongest move. The deck carries who, what, the stakes and the turn, and pairs with the headline without echoing it. The lede hooks the reader with one vivid, specific moment.
- *Source.* TH6; the director's headline note; "The title is too long. The deck is unfocused" (091826); R7.

### C6. The deliberation is its own movement

The article tells how the room reached its verdict: the theories it debated and dropped, and how the vote moved.
- *Why.* The players lived it but have never seen it from outside, and it is usually where the thesis lives.
- *Source.* D9; TL1.

### C7. Every player appears through what the record shows they did

Each player is connected to the story through something the record shows, never a roll call. Nothing is invented to place anyone, and a player the record says nothing about is raised as a question (C15).
- *Why.* Every player must see themselves.
- *Source.* PE2.

### C8. The director's lines are weighed, and the ones used are exact

The writer considers every observation, overheard line, pairing and theory in the director's notes. The article prints what the thesis uses, word for word and in the right mouth.
- *Why.* The players said those lines, and recognition is the gift.
- *Source.* D9; the director's 091826 quote correction.

### C9. Cards are receipts the room chose to expose

The article carries three to five inline cards. Each is the receipt for a claim the thesis rests on, and a memory someone chose to expose rather than sell, printed word for word. Memories come first; a document card quotes only the passage that matters. Every card is clear about what it cites. The sidebar holds five to eight entries, including every inline card.

Quote blocks quote the record word for word and name the speaker; Nova's own lines stay in prose. They are optional, and each must serve the flow: generally two or three at most, none in the lede, at most one in the closing.
- *Why.* A card is the players' own play in print: proof, recognition, the expose-or-bury choice made visible, and the most vivid text in the article. The count is the budget of an article this length, never a quota to fill.
- *Source.* F5; CA1, CA2, CA4, CA5; the director's 091826 note ("completely unclear about what it's citing"); the four cards the director restored on 092026; R1.

### C10. Every line survives tracing the mechanics

Before a vivid line stands, trace:
- who acted;
- which way the money moved;
- who profited;
- what Nova could know.

A line that only sounds right is wrong. Build the reasoning from what the record shows, and where it stops, ask: "how does a lobbyist know exactly where the money is hidden?" Name who acted wherever the record names them; "the group" or "the room" is for what the room did together: the deliberation, the vote and the verdict.
- *Source.* The director's mechanics note; the director's 071826 edits; D16f; T4; VO1; R1.

### C11. Exposures are read the way the game works

Someone surfaced each memory, often not its owner, and often for a reason. Read an exposure as an act with a motive, such as putting a memory on the board to support an accusation.
- *Source.* The director's 091826 correction on Jess's memory; EX1.

### C12. Nova is a working reporter

Nova's stake is the story: Nova had been investigating Marcus, and follows the money with receipts, calls for comment and keeps going, on the model of Coffeezilla.
- Nova never writes as one of the morning's victims and never sets Nova level with the sellers.
- One honest line about motive is allowed where the session earns it.
- Nova's conclusions are Nova's own, never put in a source's mouth.
- Voice draws on Kara Swisher, Casey Newton, Heather Cox Richardson and Marisa Kabas, and on Hunter S. Thompson for first-person energy only.
- *Source.* D5, D5a; the director's mechanics note.

### C13. Judgement lands on characters and on NeurAI

The article may hold a character's choices to account, especially where the director's notes lead there, and aims its critique at NeurAI, its board and the business of buying memories. A genuine act of grace is honoured, and still exempts no one from where the money went.
- *Source.* F4; VO3, VO4; the director's 071826 edits.

### C14. The closing

The closing is untitled and specific to this session, built from its names and its sums. It ends on an open question, never a generic op-ed. Its follow-up comes from the epilogue.
- *Source.* D12; VO2; D2.

### C15. The writer raises questions to the director

When the record holds nothing about a player, a roster pronoun is missing, or a ledger entry looks wrong, the writer raises it in a short list of questions for the director. The list is shown at the stop and is built to be skimmed. The director answers with a note. A rework keeps every question it did not answer, so an automatic pass never drops one before the director sees it (R5).
- *Source.* D8; PE2; BU5; PE1; R5.

### C16. Arcs are threads woven toward one convergence

An arc is one storyline the article is built from: its people, its evidence, its tension and its open questions. The arcs are ordered by how they bear on the room's verdict.

The arc writer examines every arc through three lenses, and notes where each supports the arc and where it cuts against it:
- the money;
- behaviour: what people did and chose;
- victimization: who was harmed, and whose memories were taken or erased.

The lenses give each section its own material, so the money section is the threads seen through the money, not a list of transactions. A lens that cuts against an arc is tension the article can use ("The room accused Vic. So where is Vic's payday?").

The threads are intercut across the sections, not told one after another. Details planted early come back changed later. At the end the threads converge: the culmination the whole article builds toward, where the thesis lands. The convergence comes near the end, never spent early in THE STORY.
- *Why.* It makes the article a narrative that culminates in a strong thesis, not a list of findings, and it is what keeps every section essential (C2).
- *Source.* TH2; R6; the director's 091826 note ("the story being told throughout the entire article using the different lenses").

## 7. What goes

These rules leave the prompts. Each is a quota or template that does not serve the article, a claim the director's decisions contradict, or example prose that leaks into articles.

- The fixed section list as a contract, with each section's fixed question. The six keys stay as slots for tooling, and the outline may leave a slot out when the thesis does its job elsewhere (TH4; C2).
- The four-part lede formula (death, memory theft, accusation preview, Nova's stake) and its "hint at both the murder AND the systemic critique"; the five-beat closing with its scale-implication step (C5; C14; VO2; R7).
- A visual every two or three paragraphs. Photos still spread through the article (PH2; C4; R8).
- The convergence placed in the middle of THE STORY with a "resolution cascade" after it, which spends the payoff before the later sections begin; and the rule that every arc appears in every later section, which turns those sections into checklists. The convergence itself stays, as the culmination near the end (C2; C16; R6).
- Nova's self-implication and "no better than the sellers", and "empathy for buriers" as a default framing (C12, C13; D5).
- The fixed Blake characterization, true of no particular session: "Nova acknowledges Blake wasn't cruel or threatening, just effective… End the Blake beat with the tension unresolved" (T15; R9).
- The fixed "preserve, do not regenerate" lines in the rework rules. The director's send-back note governs how much a rework keeps: "rethink it from scratch" gets a rethink. A rework's first line names the task its revision context gives it (TH7; R1).
- The murder framing: "the murder victim", "hint at the murder", "the murder revelation", "who killed Marcus" (T2, T15; D1).
- Relative timing only. Times are printed on the morning clock (T7).
- The whiteboard as a source (T13).
- Example prose that reads as content: real-looking account names and sums, earlier sessions, invented quotes, fixed pronouns for canonical characters, and modelled phrases that turn into tics such as "the shape of the silence". Examples use placeholders and canonical names, and teach shape, not content (DS principle 4; PE1; C4; an example card printed as content on 071826).
