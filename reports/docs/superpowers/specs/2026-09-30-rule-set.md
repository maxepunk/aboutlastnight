# The rule set

The single source for phase 3, "One rule set". Phase 3 builds the model-facing rules from this document. Every writer and judge then reads them: the arc calls, the interweaving call, the outline and article writers, their reworkers and the three judges. Phase 3 also moves into code what code can guarantee, and removes the rules listed under "What goes".

This document holds the director's decisions of 2026-09-25 to 2026-10-02:
- the rulings sheet (`docs/superpowers/specs/2026-09-25-rule-rulings.md`, cited by entry id, such as EX1);
- the phase 3 prep grill that followed, the review of the phase 3 plan, and the director's read of the gate's article, whose decisions are listed in the appendix (cited by number, such as F2 or R3).

Where the rulings sheet and this document differ, this document wins. The vocabulary is `CONTEXT.md`'s.

## 1. What the article is for

The game's own how-to-play page makes the players a promise: "a personalized investigative report on the Nova News blog, showing how your choices shaped the official story. You'll catch pieces of the narrative you may have missed during your session." The article keeps that promise for the players who were in the room. They read it to:
- see themselves, through their characters' choices, their lines and their moments of grace;
- catch what they missed: connections in the record the room never made;
- see how their choices shaped the official story: what they exposed, what they sold, what they argued, and what the verdict served.

They have to read it to the end, so it runs to about 1,500 words (F2). The article is real investigative journalism in Nova's first person, and the fiction stays whole.

## 2. The world

Every rule below uses these facts. They come from the how-to-play page and the director's confirmations (rulings sheet, "Game facts"; T3).

- **The party** happened last night. Marcus Blackwood drugged his guests and extracted their memories into memory tokens. The characters cannot remember the party; everyone, Nova included, meets it only through recovered memories.
- **The investigation** is the game itself, this morning, in the warehouse. Players unlock memory tokens, scan them and decide what each one becomes:
  - trade: give it, swap it, use it as leverage, or return it to its owner;
  - expose: turn it in to Nova; its summary goes up on the Evidence Board for everyone to see;
  - bury: sell it to be erased for ever, paid into an account the seller names; the more sensitive the memory, the more it pays. The game tells the players that NeurAI's board wants the memories gone. Inside the story the buyer is anonymous to Nova, and NeurAI is Nova's suspicion (T5; R11).
- **The Valet**, Blake, introduces himself with "I manage operations at NeurAI"; Marcus would usually call him his Valet (T3). Blake moves about the room and pulls players into quiet corners to make deals; some sales happen openly. Partway through the morning, Blake gathers everyone to take the room's temperature; the director's notes may call it the check-in, and the article tells the gathering (R16).
- **The ledger** records every transaction with its time and amount: each sale into an account, the first-burial bonus, each transfer between accounts. It never shows which memory was sold. The room sees only the running balances.
- **An account** is wherever a seller sends the money, under any name, including another character's. A shell can be shared.
- **The evidence log** records each exposure with its time and the name on the turn-in: "NovaNews (Anonymous)" or a name the player chose to put on it to take public credit (R15).
- **The group statement** is the verdict: the official story. When the recovery window closes, sales end, the police are on their way, and the room must agree one witness statement. It can cite only what is on the board, it leaves out whatever the room chooses, and it is a version every character can live with. So every choice the players made shapes it, and a buried memory can never be cited in it.
- **Nova** is an independent journalist who had been investigating Marcus. Fremont PD required Nova to monitor the investigation as an uninterested third party: the condition of Blake's deal to delay sending officers while the room investigates, agrees its statement and leaves (T3). Exposed memories are turned in to Nova. On site Nova is in the warehouse; remote, Nova monitors from outside and the room's events reach Nova from people in it. Nova takes no part in the statement.
- **Nova's day** follows the investigation: Nova writes that evening, with the follow-up reporting of the afternoon and evening. The director writes that follow-up into the notes as the epilogue.
- **There is no right answer.** The session has no answer key. The room authors the story through its choices.

### 3a. How a memory moves through the game and the data

By the end of the game every memory token has met one fate, and each fate leaves a different trace.

| Fate | What the room saw | What Nova has | What the pipeline's data holds | What the article can do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exposed | Its summary on the Evidence Board | The full memory | The memory's text; an evidence-log entry with the time and the name on the turn-in | Print it whole on a card, giving the room the full memory it saw only as a summary. Name its owner. Credit the exposer only if named (T6). Place it on the timeline |
| Buried | A balance changing on the live ledger | A ledger line: time, amount, account | The memory's text and owner, and which sale erased it | Report the sale's time, size and account; never its content or owner, except as talk the director's notes record, with the speaker named (T3) |
| Traded, held or returned | Nothing, unless people talked | Nothing, unless the director's notes record it | The memory's text, with no session entry | Nothing as evidence; talk the notes record, with the speaker named |
| Never found | Nothing | Nothing | The memory's text | Nothing. A character's absence from the board is a fact about the board, never a sign of a burial (BU1) |

Three relationships follow.
- **The ledger also holds money tied to no memory:** the first-burial bonus, and transfers between accounts.
- **The evidence log and the ledger share the game's clock.** Together they are the morning's timeline: what was going public set against what was being erased (section 6).
- **The data always knows more than Nova.** The pipeline holds every memory's text, the unfound and the buried included, and the session report says which memory each sale erased. Nova's ledger does not. So code withholds it, and a buried memory reaches the writers only as its ledger line, which phase 2 already enforces. A writer never has to hold back what it never sees.

## 4. Truth rules

Truth rules keep the article true to the record and to how the game works. Each is stated with its reason, so a writer can decide the edge cases. Every writer and every judge reads all of them. "Checked by" says what enforces the rule:
- "code" means a programmatic check;
- "judge" means the judge treats a breach as must-fix: the draft goes back for an automatic rework, within the automated budget, and the finding names the sentence and the record it contradicts. A breach is a definite error with the draft; flagging it to the director would only hand the director the same fix (R2). The claim check (phase 7) later reads every claim against the record.

### T1. Evidence decides how a claim is written

- What the record backs (an exposed memory, a document, the ledger, the evidence log, a photo), Nova states, and shows when it carries the thesis.
- What happened or was said in the room, which the director's notes record, Nova reports as the reporting mode sets out (T8), with each quoted line in its speaker's mouth (T12).
- What Nova knows or suspects but the record cannot back (Nova's earlier reporting on Marcus, who bought, who sold, why someone acted), Nova writes as a careful reporter does: as Nova's own suspicion, an allegation or a question, worded fresh each time and grounded in what the record does show.

Nothing is invented.
- *Reason.* The players know what happened, and a false detail about their character breaks the gift. Receipts set against allegations are how Nova earns belief. NovaNews answers for what it states as fact about a person or a company, and a suspicion stated as fact is libel.
- *Source.* DS principle 4; TH1; D4; T1; R12.
- *Checked by.* Judge; the claim check (phase 7).

### T2. The verdict is the group's negotiated official story

The article reports the verdict as the official story the room negotiated. It never claims to know what really happened to Marcus, and never grades the verdict against a hidden answer. A verdict with no culprit, such as an overdose or an accident, names no one. The alternative theories the room debated are always reported. How the room's choices made the story is craft (C3).
- *Reason.* The statement is a story everyone could live with, built under the clock from what was on the board. Showing how it was made is the article's promise.
- *Source.* TH5; D1, D1a, D1b, D9; the how-to-play page; R21.
- *Checked by.* Judge.

### T3. Buried memories never appear as evidence

No sentence presents what a buried memory said, or whose it was, as fact. If players talked about a memory in the room and the director's notes record it, Nova reports what was said, with the speaker named. When a memory was sold, for how much and into which account are all reportable. A sale's size may speak to what was at stake, since the buyer pays more the more sensitive the memory (R10, R11).
- *Reason.* The game erased those memories, and Nova's ledger cannot show what was sold.
- *Source.* BU3; T1.
- *Checked by.* Judge.

### T4. An account is the seller's chosen destination and name

The name is a message the seller chose: a joke, a borrowed identity, the seller's own name, or a frame. Nova reads the name for what it claims and the timing for when. A person is tied to an account as fact only when the director saw the sale, or when the sale was made openly in front of the room. Any other tie rests on something the record shows that person doing, such as being seen with Blake in the minute of a sale, and Nova poses it as a question. An account's name is never a reason to suspect its namesake. Nova's suspicion that someone sold can land on the person without naming an account, and Nova may say what the counts suggest about a player's own public claim. Totals show where the morning's money went. A transfer is a deal between players.
- *Reason.* Anyone can open an account in any name, several sellers can share one, and the live ledger does not mark whose an account is.
- *Source.* AC1, AC2, AC3; T2; R21; the director's edit of the gate article ("the numbers support Sam's claim").
- *Checked by.* Judge.

### T5. The buyer pays the seller

Money runs from the buyer to the seller's chosen account. Nova suspects the buyer is NeurAI and its board, and writes it as a suspicion (T1). The ledger's totals are the figures, and the writer computes nothing. Reportable money, when the story needs it: the first-burial bonus and transfers; each account's total; every sale's time and size; how many sales an account took; and counts of exposed memories, overall and per owner (BU2). The ledger holds the morning's payments for erasure. Money from before the party, such as an investment, a fortune or an inheritance, is the characters' motive background, from the documents. An entry that looks like a mistake goes to the director as a question (C15), not into print.
- *Reason.* Money running the wrong way is the most common factual slip, and stating the buyer as fact would accuse a company in print.
- *Source.* BU2, BU5; the director's notes on money pools; R1; R11.
- *Checked by.* Code for totals; judge for direction.

### T6. Exposers are anonymous unless named

An exposure is anonymous unless the evidence log carries a name, which is an honest attribution, or the director's notes record who turned the memory in. A memory's owner is never assumed to be its exposer. A card's citation never names the exposer.
- *Reason.* At turn-in the player chooses whether to go on the record, and anonymity protects the source, even from an on-site Nova.
- *Source.* EX1; D6; D16a.
- *Checked by.* Judge.

### T7. The stages and the clock

- **The party** exists only as memories, never as something witnessed.
- **The investigation** is told as the reporting mode allows. It ends in the group statement as the police come.
- **Nova's day** comes from the epilogue alone. The writer adds no follow-up fact or outreach result the epilogue does not give. All follow-up is Nova's own reporting, and a channel the director names (a leaked email, an anonymous tip) is Nova's source for that item. What Nova says NovaNews is still chasing is Nova's own intent, and needs no epilogue (R21).

Logged times appear on the morning clock (code, section 6). "Tonight" and "this afternoon" belong only to Nova's day.
- *Reason.* The players lived the investigation, met the party only through memories, and know nothing of the aftermath until Nova reports it.
- *Source.* TL1, TL2; D2, D3; stages (round 5).
- *Checked by.* Code for the clock; judge for the stages.

### T8. Nova is the uninterested third party

Nova never votes, joins the room's accusation or exposes a memory, and is never one of the room. On site, Nova saw and heard the investigation. Remote, the investigation reached Nova from people in the room: Nova says so once, early, as the start of Nova's questions; after that the room's events are told as scenes, with attribution where it matters (a line someone was overheard saying, a claim about a person), and Nova never claims to have seen or heard the room (R13). Nova's "we" is NovaNews, Nova and the reader, or Nova and a guest reporter; on site it may include the room only for being there.
- *Reason.* The police deal made Nova a neutral monitor, and the reporting mode sets what Nova could witness.
- *Source.* PR1 to PR4; T3; R13; R21.
- *Checked by.* Code for presence and vote claims, already in the fact check; judge.

### T9. Pronouns

Players take the pronouns on the director's roster; the pronoun set at the roster stop is the director's choice, they/them included (R3). Marcus is he/him. Blake has none invented. Nova is never referred to with gendered pronouns: Nova writes in the first person and is otherwise "Nova". A player who reaches the writers with no roster pronoun at all is raised as a question (C15), never guessed.
- *Reason.* The same character is played by different people, so canon pronouns are wrong for players. Pronoun errors were among the most frequent fixes.
- *Source.* PE1; T4.
- *Checked by.* Code for Marcus, Blake and Nova; judge for players.

### T10. The head count

When the article says how many people were in the room, it counts the players at the investigation, a subset of the party's guests. A guest reporter who shares the byline and plays a character is one of them.
- *Source.* D16b.
- *Checked by.* Code.

### T11. Characters, not players

Commentary and judgement land on the characters' choices, never on the players themselves. A player's appearance is never described.
- *Reason.* The players read about their own characters. Players are cast across genders, and a look is not a character.
- *Source.* F4; D16g.
- *Checked by.* Judge.

### T12. Words are exact

- A card copies the record: a memory whole, a document's passage. Its text carries no id or timestamp.
- A quoted line is word for word and names its real speaker, never "Nova".
- The director's lines, when used, are exact.
- *Reason.* The players wrote and said these words. A misquote, or a line in the wrong mouth, changes the story.
- *Source.* CA1, CA3, CA4; D9.
- *Checked by.* Code for cards (the fact check); judge for quotes.

### T13. Photos and the whiteboard

Every photo the director has not excluded appears; an excluded photo never does. A caption says what the photo shows and keeps the subject and action of the director's description; it may add context from the article. The whiteboard photo is never placed, and the whiteboard is never cited.
- *Reason.* The players are in the photos, and the whiteboard is the room's working notes, not a source.
- *Source.* PH1, PH2, PH3; D10, D16c, F3; R1.
- *Checked by.* Judge; caption checks in phase 9.

### T14. The fiction stays whole

No production words appear in print: director, GM, game master, tier, timer, or a bare "token" or "tokens" as a system label. "Memory token", "bury", "sold" and "memories" are the fiction's own words. There is no "Detective" receiving memories, no "Valet station" and no "Black Market" as a named institution: memories go to Nova, Blake moves about the room, and in print the market is the deals made with Blake, with no proper name; who stands behind it is Nova's suspicion (T5; R11, R18).
- *Source.* BU2, VO5; the game facts; the director's voice notes ("the Black Market" becomes the deals made with Blake).
- *Checked by.* Code for production words; judge.

### T15. Canon

Blake manages operations at NeurAI, and Marcus called Blake his Valet. Marcus is the man whose death the room investigates. What his death was is whatever the room decided (T2).
- *Source.* D7; D1.

## 5. Craft guidance

Craft is the engine; the truth rules are the floor (R19). The craft guidance teaches the genre's form as what to do and why, never as quotas or templates. The form is an anatomy of a decision with a money trail underneath. Each item says what it does for the players:
- recognition: seeing themselves through their characters' choices;
- revelation: the connections they missed;
- resonance: how their choices shaped the official story.

The guidance uses the director's vocabulary (thesis, throughline, movement, convergence) and states targets rather than prohibitions. A new judge finding on craft is should-consider. The judges' existing must-fix checks keep their status until phase 7 calibrates them (R2, R22). Each item comes from the director's words or the direction of the director's edits (rounds 4, 6 and 7). The guidance is tested in the director's next session and refined in phase 4 (R20).

The rule files group the items by the writer's job:
- craft-story: C1, C3, C16;
- craft-form: C2, C5, C6, C17, C18, C19, C14;
- craft-material: C8, C7, C10, C11;
- craft-voice: C12;
- craft-judgement: C13;
- craft-telling: C4;
- craft-cards: C9;
- craft-questions: C15.

### C1. One thesis

Before the article is planned, it has a thesis: the answer Nova will argue by the end. When the director gives one, in the notes or at a stop, that is the thesis. Otherwise it is the most compelling journalistic angle this session offers. That is usually the gap between the official story and what led up to it, as the room's choices, the record and the money show it, and what that gap reveals about the people who made the story. When the verdict fits everything the room found, the thesis is what the players' own path to the verdict shows. The question that carries the thesis opens early and runs through every section as the throughline. The thesis lands at the convergence near the end (C16). The outline names the thesis for the writer who follows it.
- *Why.* Each session's choices make a different story, and the thesis is what makes the article about this room. The question gives the players a reason to read to the end, and a thesis that reframes their own morning is the payoff.
- *Source.* F1; D1; the director's 091826 note that the thesis was not "properly defined" or "connected to the article contents"; R18; R19.

### C3. How the official story was made

The article shows how the room's choices made the official story: what surfaced and what was dropped, what was exposed and what was sold, and whose interests the story served. A pattern in the record the room did not take up is offered as what the room left on the table.
- *Why.* The room authored its own story, and showing how is the article's promise.
- *Source.* TH5; the director's 091826 headline fix; T2's former second sentence (R21).

### C16. Threads woven toward one convergence

An arc is one thread of the story: its people, its evidence, its tension and its open questions. The threads are ordered by how they bear on the room's verdict.

Each thread is examined through three lenses, noting where each lens supports it and where it cuts against it:
- the money;
- behaviour: what people did and chose;
- victimization: who was harmed, and whose memories were taken or erased.

The lenses give each section its own material, so the money section is the threads seen through the money. A lens that cuts against a thread is tension the article can use, such as the money set against the room's verdict, or what someone did set against what they claimed.

The threads are intercut through the article, not told one after another. A detail planted early comes back changed later, when its payoff moves the throughline. Near the end the threads converge and the thesis lands where they meet, said once and said sharply, in this session's names and sums. Whatever it alleges about a person or a company stays Nova's suspicion or a pointed question (T1). The arc writer and the interweaving call find the threads and where they can meet; the outline decides how the thesis lands there.
- *Why.* Woven threads make one story that builds to its thesis instead of a list of findings, and a convergence saved for the end is what the whole article pulls toward.
- *Source.* TH2; R6; R14; R19; the director's 091826 note ("the story being told throughout the entire article using the different lenses").

### C2. Every section is a movement of one story

The article is one story, and each section is a movement of it: it carries the throughline forward from its own angle and leaves the question sharper than it found it. The house sections are its usual movements:
- THE STORY: the case the room built;
- FOLLOW THE MONEY: the money moving underneath it;
- THE PLAYERS: the people and their choices;
- WHAT'S MISSING: what is still open;
- the untitled closing: where it lands.

The thesis decides which sections exist, their order and their headings. What is still open often belongs in the closing, and a section whose job is done elsewhere is dropped. The story is told through every section, never front-loaded into the first, and not every thread appears in every section.
- *Why.* An article in which every section is essential to the narrative is one the players read to the end. A first section that tells everything, followed by sections filled with leftover material, is one they skim.
- *Source.* TH4; the director's 091826 note on the front-loaded story; the director's thesis-driven note; R6; R19.

### C5. The opening: hook, turn, moment, stakes

- The headline is short: a name and an active verb carrying one specific claim, the sharpest and most human truth of this session, made for this session alone. It keeps the article's strongest move for later.
- The deck carries who, what, the stakes and the turn that tells the reader what they are really reading, with names, and pairs with the headline without echoing it.
- The lede opens on the decisive moment: one vivid, specific moment from the session that the thesis turns on.
- Within the next paragraph or two comes the nut graf: why that moment matters, what is at stake, and the question Nova is chasing.
- *Why.* The headline and the deck decide whether a player starts reading, the moment whether they keep going, and the nut graf whether they know why.
- *Source.* TH6; the director's headline notes (051726, 053026); "The title is too long. The deck is unfocused" (091826); R7; R19.

### C6. The room's story is told in scenes

The room's story is told in scenes chosen for the throughline: who did what and said what, under what pressure. Each scene goes where it does the most work, rather than in clock order. The deliberation is usually the room's decisive scene: the theories debated and dropped, and how the room came to its verdict.
- *Why.* The players lived these moments in fragments and have never seen them from outside. Told as scenes, they recognize themselves, and each scene carries the story.
- *Source.* D9; TL1; R18; R19.

### C17. Scenes set against receipts

The room's moments are set against the record: a scene, then the receipt that confirms or complicates it, whether a memory card, a document's passage or a ledger line. Where someone's claim in the room meets their own memory on the record, the two side by side are the sharpest receipt the article can print.
- *Why.* It gives recognition and revelation in one beat.
- *Source.* R19; the 092026 gate article as the director edited it (Alex's "The punch, she said, was alleged", then her own memory of the punch).

### C18. The money is the story underneath

While the room built its case, memories were being sold and erased. The section on the money tells that counter-story, the threads seen through the money (C16): what was going dark while the room argued over what had gone public, and who stood to gain. Each figure or time it prints shows something about the throughline, such as a run of sales set against the moment in the room it followed.
- *Why.* The sales were the players' secret choices. Set against the public case, they show how both shaped the official story.
- *Source.* R19; section 3 ("the money's counter-story"); the director's cut of the gate article's ledger analysis ("didn't tie into the story at all").

### C19. The question stays alive

Every section raises the stakes or complicates the question. What waits for the end is the convergence (C16). A question Nova puts to the reader is pointed and specific, asks about what happened out of the room's sight, and makes sense where it stands.
- *Why.* Momentum is what makes a true article worth reading, and a reporter who saves every thought for the end reads like a report.
- *Source.* R14; R19; the director's 053026 note on momentum; the director's cut of the gate article's questions about the vote.

### C14. The closing looks ahead

The closing looks ahead from the thesis: what is still at stake, who stands to profit, what Nova is chasing next. It is untitled and specific to this session, and its news of the evening comes from the epilogue (T7).
- *Why.* The players finish with their story still alive, and theirs alone.
- *Source.* D12; D2; R17.

### C8. The director's lines: weighed by the arc writer, used where they make sense

The arc writer weighs every observation, overheard line, pairing and theory in the director's notes, and everything in the record. The outline and the article carry what the throughline uses, each where it makes sense in its context: a line from the room in its moment, word for word and in the right mouth (T12). A line that cannot be placed so it makes sense stays out, and a connection prints when it moves the throughline.
- *Why.* The players said those lines and recognition is the gift, and a line set where it makes no sense breaks it.
- *Source.* D9; the director's 091826 quote correction; R14.

### C7. Every player appears through what they did

Each player reaches the story through something the record shows they did or said, in the section where it matters, never as a roll call. A player the record says nothing about is raised with the director (C15).
- *Why.* Every player reads the article to find themselves.
- *Source.* PE2.

### C10. Trace the mechanics before a vivid line stands

Before a vivid line stands, trace who acted, which way the money moved, who profited, and what Nova could know. The trace is the writer's own check. Build the reasoning from what the record shows, and where it stops, ask the question a reporter would ask of that gap. Name who acted wherever the record names them. "The room" is for what the room did together: the deliberation, the vote and the verdict.
- *Why.* A line that only sounds right is wrong, and the players who played the mechanics will see it.
- *Source.* The director's mechanics note; the director's 071826 edits; D16f; VO1; R1; R12.

### C11. Exposures are acts

Someone surfaced each memory, often not its owner. Putting a name on a turn-in, whoever's memory it is, is how a player takes public credit. Why someone exposed a memory is Nova's to suggest where the room's talk or the timing points to it, such as a memory going up just as an accusation formed, written as T1 sets out.
- *Why.* Choosing to expose is the players' own move, and understood the way the game works, it shows them their choices.
- *Source.* The director's 091826 correction on Jess's memory; EX1; R15.

### C12. Nova is a working reporter with a stake

Nova had been investigating Marcus and is still working the story: following the money with receipts, reporting the follow-up the epilogue gives (T7), thinking out loud from the first section on, and suspicious where the record gives reason, in a careful reporter's words (T1). The register is first person, active and alive: a reporter still working leads as they write, not a narrator delivering a verdict. Nova writes about the morning's sellers and victims as the reporter on their story. Nova's conclusions are Nova's own, never put in a source's mouth. One honest line about Nova's own motive is allowed where the session earns it. The voice draws on Kara Swisher, Casey Newton, Heather Cox Richardson and Marisa Kabas, on Coffeezilla for following the money, and on Hunter S. Thompson for first-person energy only.
- *Why.* The players read a reporter on their story, and a reporter's stake and suspicion give it momentum.
- *Source.* D5, D5a; the director's mechanics note; the director's 053026 note on the register; R19.

### C13. Judgement lands on characters; the critique reaches past them

The article may hold a character's choices to account, especially where the director's notes lead there. Where its critique reaches past the characters, it aims at NeurAI, its board and the business of buying memories, written as T5 sets out. A genuine act of grace is honoured, and still exempts no one from where the money went.
- *Why.* The players read to see their characters' choices weighed, grace included, and the critique of the business lands where this session's evidence leads.
- *Source.* F4; VO3, VO4; the director's 071826 edits; R11; R18.

### C4. About 1,500 words, every paragraph pulling the reader on

The article runs to about 1,500 words, and the thesis decides what earns space. Every paragraph and card pulls the reader into the next, and every sharp line makes a claim about this session. Recaps, scaffolding, lines that announce what is coming and lines that could sit in any article are cut. The players know how the game works because they played it. Where the record stops on something that matters, Nova says so in one plain line and lets it lead into the suspicion or the question it raises, and explains no more of the game than that. House style puts a comma, a colon or a full stop where an em-dash might go; code checks for em-dashes (DT1), and code flags the narrator's prose (headline, deck and paragraphs) above 1,800 words, as an advisory (R4).
- *Why.* Players stop reading long articles and flat paragraphs, and a game explained to the people who played it is a paragraph they skip.
- *Source.* TH3; F2; BU4; the director's 091826 note ("each paragraph and evidence card should be designed to make the reader want to continue reading the next"); the director's cuts on 091826, 071826 and the gate article; R1; R4; R12.

### C9. Cards are receipts the room chose to expose

The article carries three to five inline cards. Each is the receipt for a claim the thesis rests on, and a memory someone chose to expose rather than sell, printed word for word. Memories come first; a document card quotes only the passage that matters. Every card is clear about what it cites. The sidebar holds five to eight entries, including every inline card.

Quote blocks quote the record word for word and name the speaker; Nova's own lines stay in prose. They are optional, and each must serve the flow: generally two or three at most, none in the lede, at most one in the closing.
- *Why.* A card is the players' own play in print: proof, recognition, the expose-or-bury choice made visible, and the most vivid text in the article. The count is the budget of an article this length, never a quota to fill.
- *Source.* F5; CA1, CA2, CA4, CA5; the director's 091826 note ("completely unclear about what it's citing"); the four cards the director restored on 092026; R1.

### C15. The writer raises questions to the director

When the record holds nothing about a player, a roster pronoun is missing, or a ledger entry looks wrong, the writer raises it in a short list of questions for the director. The list is shown at the stop and is built to be skimmed. The director answers with a note. A rework keeps every question it did not answer, so an automatic pass never drops one before the director sees it (R5).
- *Source.* D8; PE2; BU5; PE1; R5.
